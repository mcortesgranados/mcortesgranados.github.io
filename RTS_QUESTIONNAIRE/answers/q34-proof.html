34. Distributed systems

Answer as submitted

“Yes — distributed systems are the core of my architecture experience, across enterprise middleware, microservices and cloud-native platforms.”
Technical decision: expand. The submitted line claims the experience without showing a system. This page is the separate, detailed version: it shows the division into bounded contexts, the synchronous and asynchronous contracts, the resilience pipeline with its ordering explained, and the operational properties that decide whether a service can actually be run.

Expanded answer

Yes, across enterprise middleware and cloud-native platforms. The division follows bounded contexts rather than layering: services own their data, no database is shared, and the event-driven backbone between contexts replaces the synchronous chain that would otherwise couple their availability. Synchronous calls are used only where the caller genuinely needs the answer now, and every one carries a deadline and a retry budget, because a slow dependency without a deadline becomes thread-pool exhaustion in the caller. Asynchronous messaging carries the rest: a schema, a version and an ordering key, partitioned by aggregate id so ordering is per entity rather than global, with the consumer acknowledging only after the side effect is committed. Reliability is a pipeline rather than a collection of tricks — timeout, bulkhead, retry with jitter, circuit breaker, dead-letter queue — whose order is the design, wrapped around consumers that are idempotent by construction because at-least-once is a property of the transport and cannot be negotiated away. Operability is what makes it real: stateless services, scale signals that predict load rather than merely exist (queue depth for consumers, not CPU), rollouts that drain in-flight work, and one correlation id following a request across every hop. The failures I plan for are the unglamorous ones: messages arriving twice, arriving out of order, a consumer killed mid-message, and a dependency that is slow rather than down.

What the distributed design buys

Rules I apply. Let the boundary decide the service rather than the org chart, and give each service its own data so no one is tempted into a distributed join; put a deadline on every synchronous call, because an unbounded wait is a resource leak with a dependency attached; state the delivery guarantee explicitly and assume at-least-once, since exactly-once is not on offer; order the resilience strategies deliberately, because a retry outside its breaker and its bulkhead amplifies an outage instead of absorbing it; make every consumer idempotent and version-aware so redelivery and reordering are ordinary events; and require each alert to have a dashboard, a runbook and a named owner, because an alert nobody can act on is noise that trains the team to ignore the page.
