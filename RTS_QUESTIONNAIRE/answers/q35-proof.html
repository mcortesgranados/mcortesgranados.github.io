35. Distributed transactions

Answer as submitted

“Yes — I have designed and implemented consistency across multiple services and databases, using saga-style coordination rather than fragile two-phase commit.”
Technical decision: expand. The submitted line names the pattern and the rejection. This page is the separate, detailed version: it shows the saga with its state and compensations, why exactly-once is a property of the effect rather than of the delivery, how the outbox removes the need for a distributed lock, and the reconciliation job that exists because compensation is best-effort by nature.

Expanded answer

Yes, and the honest framing is that I do not use distributed transactions across service boundaries at all. Across services I use a saga: a long-running process modelled as a sequence of local transactions, each paired with a compensating action designed at the same time as the step rather than after the incident. Orchestration when the process has more than a handful of steps or a business owner who needs to read it, choreography when the flow is genuinely simple and the participants are already event-driven. The saga state is persisted after every step, so a process that dies mid-way resumes rather than silently stopping, and a participant timeout is treated as a failure because an unbounded wait turns a saga into an orphan holding a lock nobody releases. The transactional outbox is what makes the forward path reliable without a distributed lock: the state change and the outgoing event commit in one local transaction, and a relay publishes afterwards. Exactly-once is a property of the effect, not of the delivery — the transport is at-least-once, so the effect is made idempotent three times over with a client key, a unique constraint as the arbiter, and a conditional write. Finally I accept eventual consistency and engineer around it: read-your-writes where a user would notice the lag, a reconciliation job that compares both sides of every process and repairs the divergences, and a DLQ with an owner. Two-phase commit still has a place inside a single transactional resource; across a service boundary it would make every participant's availability depend on the slowest one.

What the approach buys

Rules I apply. Design the compensation with the step, because reverse-engineering the undo during an incident is how money gets lost; persist the saga state after every step, since a process that cannot resume is a process that silently stops; treat a participant timeout as a failure, because waiting forever is how a lock is never released; push non-compensable steps after the last point of failure, since an email cannot be unsent; make the effect idempotent three times over and let the database be the arbiter, because the transport is at-least-once no matter what the documentation promises; and keep a reconciliation job with a real alert, because eventual consistency without reconciliation is just hoping.
