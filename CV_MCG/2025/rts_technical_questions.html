Q1 Opening Motivation

Tell me about yourself — and why cargo revenue management?

Question: “Give me a short introduction, and tell me why you are interested in this role and in our cargo business.”
What he is really testing: whether you can summarise twenty-eight years in ninety seconds without listing technologies, and whether the interest in cargo is thought through rather than simply available enthusiasm.
Show answer · 90-second structure, the cargo hook, what to leave out

Three movements, then stop: what I do, the problem I solve repeatedly, and why this business.

What I do. “I am a software engineer with 28 years in enterprise systems — mostly .NET back ends, Angular front ends, integrations and data — always on systems where the numbers have to be right: financial calculations, regulatory reporting, operational planning.”

The problem I solve well. “My strongest pattern is taking a process a business team runs on spreadsheets and tribal knowledge, and turning it into a system they trust — rules explicit, data reconciled, decisions auditable. That is the work I enjoy, because the value shows up in the client's numbers, not in a demo.”

Why cargo. “Cargo is the version of revenue management where the product is not homogeneous: weight, volume, routing, allotments and rating all interact, and a mistake costs margin or breaks a contract. It is also a product line with decades of established behaviour, so I would rather learn it from people who know it than work on something generic.”

Say it like this: “I turn processes that live in spreadsheets into systems a business team trusts, and cargo is where that skill has the most direct effect on margin.”
One line of proof, if asked: “The stack is familiar — .NET 8 with layered services, Angular for planner screens, SQL Server with EF Core or Dapper — so my learning curve is the domain, not the technology.”
If he pushes: Which part of your experience transfers fastest? · What do you want to learn in the first six months? · Have you worked with transport or logistics clients before?
Q2 Cargo revenue management Plain language

What does revenue management mean in cargo? Explain it as you would to a client.

Question: “You would be working on cargo revenue management products. Tell me what you understand by that, in your own words — no need for formulas.”
What he is really testing: whether you reach for the business meaning or recite textbook jargon, and whether you know cargo is not passenger seats: the unit is a kilogram and a cubic metre, and the customer is often a forwarder with a contract.
Show answer · capacity, price per kg, commitments, the two expensive mistakes

“Every flight that departs has capacity that disappears the moment the door closes — and it is sold in kilograms and cubic metres, not seats. Revenue management is how we decide who gets that space, at what price, and how much we hold back for better-paying demand that may still arrive.”

Three things make cargo different, and I would say them explicitly:

  • Capacity is two-dimensional. A shipment consumes weight and volume at the same time, sometimes a specific container position; whichever runs out first is the real constraint. So “we have space” is never a single number.
  • Much of the space is already promised. Allotments and block-space agreements reserve capacity whether the customer uses it or not, so the commercial question is not only “what price” but “what did we commit to, and what is genuinely free to sell”.
  • The product has a route and a deadline. A shipment can be split, re-routed or lost to a competitor because it missed a cut-off. Availability and pricing have to respect the physical journey, not just the leg.

Then the value: the software exists to reduce the two expensive mistakes — selling capacity too cheaply too early, and departing with space we could have sold. When that trade-off is visible, repeatable and defensible to a client, the system has done its job.

Say it like this: “Cargo revenue management is deciding who gets space that expires, when it is measured in kilograms and cubic metres and much of it is already committed by contract — and the software makes that decision repeatable instead of personal.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Technically that means a capacity model carrying weight, volume and ULD positions together, and a rating engine deterministic enough to reproduce an invoice six weeks later.”
If he pushes: What would you look at first in a cargo P&L? · What is hardest about cargo forecasting, in your view? · Where does cargo lag passenger revenue management?
Q3 Diagnosis Client outcome

A client says volumes are growing but revenue is flat. How do you investigate?

Question: “One of our cargo customers tells us volumes are up but revenue has not moved. They are pressing us to ‘improve the system’. Where do you start?”
What he is really testing: whether you jump to a technical answer or first establish what the business is seeing. A VP wants someone who protects the client from a wrong fix as much as someone who ships.
Show answer · hypothesis tree, mix versus yield, what to ask, how to prove it

“I would resist changing the system until we know which story is true, because the fix is different in each case.”

The decomposition I would put on a whiteboard, in business language:

  • Mix. Are the extra kilograms low-yield traffic — cheap commodities, promotional rates, contract renewals at lower rates? Volume up with revenue flat is often mix, not system failure.
  • Yield per kilogram by lane and account. If one large forwarder renegotiated, the aggregate hides it. Split revenue by route, commodity and customer before touching a line of code.
  • Utilisation and allocation. Are we flying fuller but giving away space we should have protected? Are allotments under-used while free sale is closed?
  • Data quality. The last possibility, and the one that wastes the most time when ignored: are we measuring the same thing? Missing shipments, duplicated AWBs, cut-off timing or currency would produce exactly this symptom.

My next step is a working session with their commercial team plus one data extract — a month of shipments and the last two rate reviews — and come back with three views: revenue per kilogram by lane, utilisation versus allotment, and top accounts year on year. Usually the conversation changes there. Sometimes the request becomes “help us see this monthly”, which is a cheaper and more valuable deliverable than a new optimizer.

Say it like this: “Before changing the system I would separate mix from yield from allocation using their own data, because two of those three are commercial decisions and only one is ours to fix in software.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That starts as a reconciliation: shipment counts and revenue by lane, so we know the numbers agree before drawing conclusions from them.”
If he pushes: What if the data cannot be reconciled quickly? · How do you tell a client the problem is their pricing? · What would you deliver in the first two weeks?
Q4 Requirements Users

Our users are analysts, not engineers. How do you get from their complaint to something we can build?

Question: “Our revenue analysts and planners know the business but they do not write specifications. How do you turn what they tell you into software that actually helps them?”
What he is really testing: whether you have sat next to a real user. The jump from “they asked for a button” to “here is the decision they are making and the data it needs” is what separates a developer from someone who can own a client relationship.
Show answer · observe the decision, prototype with real data, acceptance in business terms

“I start by watching the work, not by asking for requirements. The request people write down is usually their proposed solution; the useful information is in the spreadsheet they keep next to it, because that is the tool they actually trust.”

Concretely, four steps:

  • Observe one real cycle. Sit with an analyst through a full run — the report they open, the exceptions they chase, the call they make at the end. I write down the decision, the inputs it depends on, and what they do when a number looks wrong.
  • Take their artefact seriously. If they export to Excel and add three columns, those three columns are the requirement. Reproducing them in the product is usually the fastest path to trust.
  • Prototype on their data. One screen with real numbers and a “does this match what you expect?” conversation beats a document. Disagreements surface in minutes, and the analyst becomes a co-designer.
  • Agree the acceptance in business terms. “The morning check takes ten minutes instead of two hours, and the exceptions list matches the one you build manually” — not “the grid supports filtering”.

I also make the trade-off explicit early: what we are not doing in this version, and what they will keep doing in Excel for now. That single sentence prevents most of the disappointment later, and it is usually the thing the client remembers you for.

Say it like this: “I watch one real cycle, take their spreadsheet as the specification, prototype on their own data, and agree the acceptance in the language of their morning routine.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Angular-side that usually means server-side paging and a grid built for keyboard work, plus an export that matches what they already send to their commercial team.”
If he pushes: What do you do when two clients want contradictory behaviour? · How do you handle a user who cannot articulate the rule? · How much of this can be done remotely?
Q5 Product judgement Customization

One carrier wants something that could break the product for everyone else.

Question: “A large customer asks for a change that makes sense for them but would complicate the product for the rest of the clients. They are important. What do you do?”
What he is really testing: whether you can hold a product line together while keeping a key account. In a company of this size, one unmanaged customization can cost years of maintenance — and one clumsy refusal can cost a client.
Show answer · need versus solution, configuration versus fork, how to say no

“First I separate the need from the requested solution, because the request is usually one idea among several that would solve the same problem.”

Then a short decision path I would take to the product owner, not decide alone:

  • Is the underlying need shared? If two or three other carriers have the same pain, this is a product feature with a general design, and the requesting client becomes the pilot.
  • Can it be configuration? Rates, rules, thresholds and workflows are legitimate places for per-client variation. If the difference is data and policy rather than logic, configuration is the cheap answer — as long as it is designed once and tested.
  • If it is truly unique, it is a funded extension. Code paths per client are a maintenance tax that outlives the deal: named owner, tests, documentation, and a conversation about who pays for it and for how long.
  • Say no with an alternative. “We will not branch the core, but here is what we can do in this release, and here is the workaround for the next two quarters.” A no with a date is a plan; a no without one is a rejection.

The part I would not skip is writing the decision down — what was asked, what we chose, what we gave up — because in two years someone will ask why the product behaves this way, and the answer should not depend on memory.

Say it like this: “I separate the need from the requested solution, look for configuration before code, and if it is genuinely unique I make it a funded extension with an owner — and I say no with a date and an alternative, never a bare no.”
One line of proof, if asked: “The technical version of that rule is: client-specific variation lives in configuration or a plugin boundary with its own tests, never in branches inside shared code.”
If he pushes: How do you handle the sales pressure? · Who should own that decision? · Have you ever removed a customization?
Q6 Data trust Client confidence

Airline data arrives late, incomplete or duplicated. How do you protect the revenue decision?

Question: “Our data comes from schedules, reservation systems, fare files and revenue accounting — and some of it is late, missing or inconsistent. How do you stop that from producing bad decisions or, worse, bad advice to the client?”
What he is really testing: whether you understand that the danger is not a crashed job but a successful run on wrong data. That distinction is the whole conversation.
Show answer · fail loudly, reconcile, show freshness, alert the client

“The dangerous case is not a failure — it is a run that completes with incomplete data and publishes numbers nobody questions. So my first rule is that missing input stops the decision, rather than becoming a zero.”

In practice, four habits:

  • Validate at the door. Expected record counts, date ranges and mandatory fields. A schedule file short by a hundred flights should reject, not load partially — a partial schedule produces a confident, wrong plan.
  • Reconcile, then publish. Compare what arrived against what was expected, per source and per day, and write that report where a human can see it. A feed that stopped at two in the morning looks exactly like a quiet day unless someone counts.
  • Show freshness in the product. The screen should say “as of last night's cycle”, and where a figure is estimated or carried forward, say so. Analysts forgive a known gap; they do not forgive a silent one.
  • Tell the client early. If a decision is based on incomplete data, that is a conversation with the account team the same day, not a footnote in a release note.

Where a rule must tolerate missing data — a new route with no history, a capacity record not yet loaded — I prefer an explicit business default that is visible and reviewable over a silent fallback. And after every incident of this kind, the fix is a check plus an alert, not “we will be more careful”.

Say it like this: “Bad data is more dangerous when the job succeeds than when it fails, so missing input stops the decision, and the count is always reconciled before anything is published.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That is idempotent loading with a business key, a staging and quarantine path, and a daily reconciliation report with an alert on divergence.”
If he pushes: What do you do when the client's own feed is wrong? · How do you handle a time-zone or cut-off ambiguity? · Which of these would you automate first?
Q7 Modernization Business risk

Some of our products have been in production since the nineties. How would you modernize one without risking a client's operation?

Question: “We have long-lived products in production for major carriers. If you were asked to modernize one, how would you do it without putting a client's operation at risk?”
What he is really testing: whether you think in slices and reversibility, or in rewrites. With twenty years of client history behind the product, the fear is not the technology — it is losing behaviour nobody documented.
Show answer · small slices, pilot client, parallel run, reversibility, visible value

“I would treat the running system as the specification and never take a big step without a way back. The risk is not that the new code is worse — it is that a rule which only exists as behaviour gets quietly lost.”

How that looks in practice:

  • Freeze the behaviour first. Capture real inputs and the outputs the client accepted, and keep them as a comparison set. Any new version has to reproduce them before it is allowed near a client.
  • One slice at a time. Start where the value is visible and the risk is low — a report, one screen, one market type — rather than at the database or the framework.
  • Pilot with one willing client. Run both paths in parallel, compare the numbers daily, and let the client see the comparison. Their confidence is the real acceptance test.
  • Keep the switch reversible. Per client and per capability, so a problem is a switch back, not an incident call at midnight. A migration you can only move forwards is not controlled.
  • Respect their calendar. No cutover near a schedule change or a peak season; the client's commercial year sets the release dates, not ours.

And I would be honest about pace: this is a programme measured in quarters, with each step worth something on its own. If the first release is only infrastructure, the client loses interest and the funding disappears before the value arrives.

Say it like this: “Treat the live system as the specification, move one slice at a time with a pilot client, compare the numbers in parallel, and keep every step reversible — the client's calendar sets the pace.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That is a strangler approach with an adapter in front of the old schema, database changes done as add, backfill and remove in separate releases, and API versions so clients do not move in lockstep.”
If he pushes: How do you get the comparison data out of a system nobody fully understands? · When do you retire the old path? · What would make you stop the programme?
Q8 Support Client trust

Something breaks during a client's peak week. What do you do, in what order?

Question: “A carrier calls on a Monday morning in their busiest week: the numbers on the planning screen are wrong and they have decisions to make today. Walk me through your first hour.”
What he is really testing: whether you protect the client before you satisfy your own curiosity, and whether you communicate while you work. In a company this size, the second is often what the client remembers.
Show answer · stop the bleeding, communicate, restore, then fix and prevent

“First hour, in this order: stop the damage, tell them what we know, restore a trustworthy number, and only then investigate.”

  • Stop the bleeding. If the last published decision is still valid, revert to it or switch the affected capability off. A client can work with yesterday's numbers; they cannot work with numbers they do not trust.
  • Communicate with a named owner and a cadence. One person on our side owns the client conversation, with an update every thirty minutes even when there is nothing new. Silence is what turns a technical incident into a relationship problem.
  • Restore, then diagnose. Confirm the data path first — a feed that arrived late produces the same symptom as a logic bug — and be explicit about which figures are safe to use.
  • Fix the data, carefully. Any correction in the client's production data runs with a reviewed statement, a before-and-after count and a record of what changed. An unaudited data fix is indistinguishable from corruption afterwards.
  • Prevent it visibly. A monitoring check plus an alert before the client notices, and a short written review of what happened and what changed — no blame, just the missing check.

The judgement I would show is about expectations: if the root cause will take two days, say two days on the first call rather than a hopeful “soon”. Clients forgive a hard truth early far more easily than a soft promise that slips.

Say it like this: “Revert to a number they can trust, put one named owner on the client with updates every half hour, then diagnose — and any data correction comes with a count and a record.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That requires severity levels agreed in advance and a runbook per product — symptom, checks in order, rollback step, who to call — written before the peak, not during it.”
If he pushes: What if the client's team caused it? · How do you keep the account team informed? · What do you change afterwards so it does not repeat?
Q9 AI Risk and value

Where does AI genuinely help this business — and where does it create risk?

Question: “Everyone is being asked about AI. In our context — airline and cargo clients, pricing logic, sensitive commercial data — where do you see real value, and what would you refuse to do?”
What he is really testing: whether you can separate useful productivity from a demo. A credible answer names a concrete task, a limit, and who approves the output.
Show answer · concrete uses, hard limits, governance in one breath

“Value first, and it is mostly in time saved around the work rather than in the decision itself.”

Where I have seen it genuinely help:

  • Reading and summarising. Logs, incident timelines, long requirement threads and legacy documentation — a first draft in seconds that a person then corrects.
  • Test and query drafting. Generating test cases for edge conditions I might forget, or a first version of a reporting query that an analyst then reviews against the real schema.
  • Explaining code and rules. Making an unfamiliar module or an undocumented calculation readable, which is exactly the bottleneck in long-lived products.
  • Support triage. Classifying incoming tickets and drafting the first reply, always with a human sending it.

What I would refuse or restrict: client commercial data in public tools without an approved path; generated pricing or allocation logic promoted without validation against known outcomes; and anything that writes to a production database on its own. In this domain a plausible-looking rule is more dangerous than an obvious error, because it survives review.

The governance I would propose is simple enough to actually be followed: approved tools only, no client identifiers leaving the environment, generated code reviewed like anyone else's, and a named person accountable for every output that reaches a client. Then measure whether it saved time — if it does not show up in cycle time or defect rate, it is entertainment.

Say it like this: “It buys time on reading, drafting and explaining — not on deciding. Nothing generated touches a client's pricing logic or data without a named person validating it.”
One line of proof, if asked: “I use it daily for tests, refactors and reading unfamiliar code, and the rule I apply to my own work is that a generated change is reviewed line by line and covered by a test that fails without it.”
If he pushes: Have you seen it produce something wrong that passed review? · How would you introduce it to a sceptical team? · Where would AI help our analysts specifically?
Q10 Ways of working Distributed team

Dallas, the UK, South Africa, India — and clients in other time zones. How do you keep a team aligned?

Question: “Our teams and clients are spread across several countries. How do you work in that setup without things falling between the cracks?”
What he is really testing: whether you have actually worked distributed, and whether you write things down. In a cargo VP's world, the cost of an unclear decision is a client escalation three weeks later.
Show answer · written by default, one owner, overlap, mentoring, client language

“I assume overlap is scarce and make the written record carry the coordination.”

  • Decisions written in one page. Context, options, what we chose, what we gave up. Anything agreed on a call goes back into writing the same day, because a decision that lives only in a call disappears at the next handover.
  • One owner per topic. Not a committee. The owner is whoever can answer “why is it like this?” six months later, and they are named in the ticket.
  • Small, reviewable pieces. Work sized so a colleague in another time zone can review it in one sitting; a pull request nobody can finish before their evening is a blocked day, not a code review.
  • Protect the overlap hour. Use it for the conversations that need nuance — a client issue, a design argument, feedback — and keep status out of meetings entirely.
  • Mentoring by default. In distributed teams juniors get less accidental learning, so it has to be deliberate: pairing sessions, review comments that explain the why, and a named person for each new joiner.

With clients, the same discipline plus one extra rule: say what you know, say what you do not, and give a time when you will. “I do not know yet, I will confirm by four your time” protects credibility far better than a fast guess — and in an English-speaking client relationship, clarity matters more than perfect grammar.

Say it like this: “Written by default, one named owner per decision, small reviewable pieces, and the overlap hour reserved for things that need a conversation.”
One line of proof, if asked: “In practice that is trunk-based work with short branches, pipeline checks that cannot be skipped, and the reason for each change captured in the pull request.”
If he pushes: How do you handle a disagreement across regions? · What do you do when a client wants a daily call? · How do you onboard someone remotely?

Questions to ask himChosen for a cargo VP, not generic ones

  • First 90 daysWhat would a strong first three months look like in this role — and what would tell you the hire was the right one?
  • Cargo roadmapWhere is cargo revenue management heading in the next three years: pricing, automation, or better integration with the carriers' own systems?
  • Client gapWhat is the biggest gap today between what carriers ask for and what the product already does?
  • The teamHow is the cargo technology team organised across Dallas, the UK, South Africa and India, and where would this role sit?
  • Stack realityWhat does the stack look like in practice today, and what is already planned to change?
  • CustomizationHow do you decide today between a client-specific extension and something that goes into the product?
  • AI in practiceIs AI being used inside RTS today — in the products, or in how the teams work?
  • Success signalWhat is the one thing that, if we did not fix it in the next year, would hurt the cargo business most?

Pick three. In a thirty-minute slot there is room for three questions and one follow-up each; asking eight signals that you were not listening. The first, the third and the eighth are the highest-value ones for a VP.
