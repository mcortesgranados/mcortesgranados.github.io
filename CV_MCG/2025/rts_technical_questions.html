Q1 Revenue management Business model

Explain revenue management in two minutes — and where does software actually sit in it?

Question: “RTS has been selling revenue management, scheduling and pricing solutions to airlines, ferry and rail operators since 1991. Forget code for a moment: what is revenue management, what levers does it pull, and where does our software create the value?”
What is being probed: whether the candidate understands the business the code serves. The tell is language: capacity as a perishable asset, displacement cost, forecast feeding an optimizer feeding availability. A candidate who reduces it to “dynamic pricing” has the vocabulary of e-commerce, not of transport.
Show answer · definition, levers, leg vs O&D, where software fits

Revenue management is the practice of selling a fixed, perishable inventory to the right segment at the right time at the right price. A seat, a cabin, a ferry deck space or a rail segment that departs empty has zero residual value, and the cost of carrying one more passenger is close to zero, so the entire margin question is how capacity is allocated across willingness to pay. Software sits in the middle of that loop, not beside it: it forecasts demand, decides how much inventory to protect for higher-value demand, prices and makes availability visible, and then measures whether the decision worked.

The levers, in the order I would describe them to a non-technical stakeholder:

  • Forecasting — unconstrained demand per market, cabin and departure, at a given point before departure. Everything downstream is only as good as this number.
  • Inventory control — how many units to sell at each fare level, which is where nested buckets, protection levels and bid price (the marginal value of one more unit of capacity) live.
  • Overbooking — selling above capacity to cover no-shows, balanced against denied-boarding cost and regulation.
  • Pricing — fare structure, fences and restrictions, and increasingly continuous pricing; the fare is the product, the inventory control decides who can buy it.
  • Optimization — the network view: on an airline, a passenger flows over several legs, so the value of a seat is O&D displacement cost, not a leg-local price. That is the difference between leg-based and network (O&D) revenue management, and it is the part that makes the problem genuinely hard.

Software therefore does four jobs: a batch cycle (forecast, optimize, publish controls) that must finish inside the airline's planning window, a real-time availability service that answers in milliseconds and must never contradict the published controls, a scenario and what-if layer so analysts can test a decision before committing it, and a reporting layer that closes the loop on load factor, yield and RASK.

The value statement I would make in the interview: the software does not invent revenue, it reduces the two expensive mistakes — selling cheap too early and departing with empty capacity — and it makes the trade-off visible and repeatable instead of depending on one analyst's intuition.

Strong signal: perishable capacity, displacement cost, forecast → optimizer → availability as one loop, and the leg-based versus O&D distinction raised without prompting.
Likely follow-ups: How would you explain bid price to an airline analyst? · Why can a network control beat a leg control? · What is the cost of a forecast that is biased low? · Which KPI would you watch after a pricing change?
Q2 Forecasting Data pipeline

Build the demand forecasting pipeline behind a revenue management system.

Question: “Everything in our products starts with a forecast. Walk me through how you would build and operate that pipeline: the data you need, the granularity, how you deal with incomplete history, and how you know the forecast is any good.”
What is being probed: data engineering judgement in a domain where the history is censored. The classic failure is forecasting from bookings alone and never noticing that demand was capped by the controls the system itself published.
Show answer · sources, granularity, censoring, backtesting, ops

I would start from the decision the forecast feeds, and work backwards. The optimizer needs demand per market (O&D or leg), cabin, fare class and departure, at a given point before departure — the usual cut is by days-to-departure bucket, because demand behaviour close to departure is a different regime from demand ninety days out.

Inputs. Bookings and cancellations with their transaction date (to reconstruct the booking curve), the schedule and any changes to it, fares and fare rules, capacity by cabin, no-show history, and the events that break the pattern: holidays, school breaks, major events, competitor schedule changes, strikes, weather. Station and market metadata matter too, because the same city pair behaves differently by season and by day of week.

The censoring problem is the heart of it. Bookings are the realised demand, capped by the availability we published; if we closed a cheap bucket, the missing bookings are not absent demand. I handle it by combining bookings with refused/regret data where it exists, by unconstraining the observed bookings under the controls that were in force, and by keeping the controls themselves as features, so the model can distinguish “nobody wanted it” from “we did not sell it”. Without that step the system learns to forecast its own restrictions — a feedback loop that quietly flattens revenue.

Model shape. A pick-up model per bucket — how much will still be sold between now and departure — is usually more defensible than a single black-box regressor, and it is explainable to an analyst. Seasonality, day-of-week and lead time enter as structure; a gradient-boosted model can be layered on residuals where the data supports it. I keep the model versioned with the dataset and the feature set that produced it, because “the forecast changed” must be explainable months later.

Evaluation. Backtesting on rolling windows, scored with bias as well as error: MAPE and RMSE tell me the size of the miss, but a forecast that is consistently 4% low is a revenue leak even with a decent MAPE. I also track the decision metric — simulated revenue or load factor against the actual controls — because a forecast can improve statistically and still make the optimizer worse.

Operations. Late schedule changes, cancellations of whole flights, duplicated bookings, station codes changing and time-zone shifts are normal, not edge cases: the pipeline needs reconciliation counts per run, a quarantine path for records that fail validation, idempotent reloads so a rerun does not double count, and a freshness SLA the batch can be measured against.

Strong signal: unconstraining and the feedback loop named explicitly; bias treated as a first-class metric; the model tied to the decision it feeds; reconciliation and idempotency treated as part of the design.
Likely follow-ups: How do you forecast a new route with no history? · What do you do when the schedule changes after the batch ran? · How would you detect data drift before the analyst does? · Where would Python fit next to a .NET pipeline?
Q3 Operations research Optimization

Where does operations research earn its place — and how do you keep an optimizer inside the operational window?

Question: “Operations research is one of our capabilities. Tell me where you would use a formal optimization model rather than a heuristic, and then how you make it finish in time and keep it trustworthy for the planners who use it.”
What is being probed: engineering realism around solvers. Anyone can call a solver; the judgement is in problem formulation, decomposition, time limits, and what happens when the model returns a worse answer than yesterday.
Show answer · formulation, LP vs heuristic, time budget, guardrails

I use a formal model when the constraints interact — capacity shared across legs or crossings, allotments, crew or fleet continuity, minimum connection times — because that is exactly where a greedy rule silently sub-optimises. I use a heuristic when the decision is separable, the data is thin, or the deadline is measured in seconds; a rule that a planner can predict is often worth more than two percent of theoretical optimality.

The formulations I would expect to meet in this domain:

  • Network revenue optimization — a linear program over an O&D network whose duals become bid prices; the duals are the product, not a by-product, so numerical stability matters.
  • Capacity allocation and overbooking — protection levels per fare class, with a stochastic element for no-shows.
  • Scheduling and fleet assignment — mixed-integer, where the model is large and the practical answer comes from decomposition or a column-generation style approach.
  • Pricing and fare structure — often a search over discrete options with an elasticity model rather than a single closed-form optimum.

On the time budget I work backwards from the window, not forwards from the model: if the batch must publish controls before the commercial day starts, the solver gets a hard wall-clock limit and everything around it — data prep, post-processing, publishing — is budgeted too. Then the techniques that make it fit: aggregate markets before solving and disaggregate afterwards, warm-start from the previous solution, re-optimize only the affected sub-network when a schedule change touches a few markets, run independent scenarios in parallel with bounded concurrency, and always keep the last good solution so a failed run is not an outage.

// Optimization run as a job, with a budget and a safe fallback — not a request thread
public async Task<RunResult> RunAsync(OptimizationRequest req, CancellationToken ct)
{
    var lastGood = await _runs.GetLastPublishedAsync(req.NetworkId, ct);
    using var budget = CancellationTokenSource.CreateLinkedTokenSource(ct);
    budget.CancelAfter(req.TimeBudget);                 // wall clock, set by the operating window
    try
    {
        var solution = await _solver.SolveAsync(req, budget.Token);
        return await _publisher.PublishAsync(solution, ct);   // validated before it becomes live
    }
    catch (OperationCanceledException) when (!ct.IsCancellationRequested)
    {
        _log.Warning("Optimization exceeded {Budget}; keeping previous controls", req.TimeBudget);
        return RunResult.TimedOut(lastGood);            // degrade, do not fail the cycle
    }
}

Trust is the other half. An optimizer that produces a number a planner cannot explain gets switched off, so I keep three guardrails: input validation (a missing capacity record must fail the run loudly, not become a zero), sanity checks on the output (bid prices within a plausible band, no market losing all availability), and explainability — the binding constraints and the top contributors behind a change, so the analyst can accept or reject with reasons. Changes above a threshold go out as a proposal for review rather than straight to production.

Strong signal: a clear rule for LP versus heuristic, duals understood as the deliverable, decomposition and warm starts used for the time budget, and a deliberate fallback when the run times out.
Likely follow-ups: How do you validate a solver before trusting it? · What breaks when demand is sparse? · How would you version and reproduce a published solution? · When would you tell a client that the model is the wrong tool?
Q4 Integrations Schedules · GDS · accounting

Our products sit between reservation systems and airline operations. How do you integrate without losing or duplicating a single message?

Question: “Revenue management is only as good as the data it receives. We integrate with reservation and departure-control systems, schedule feeds, fare data and revenue accounting, and some of those sources are files on a schedule rather than an API. How do you design that so nothing is lost, duplicated or silently stale?”
What is being probed: distributed-data discipline in an environment that is partly file-based and partly real-time. The expected answer separates delivery semantics, data identity and reconciliation — three different problems often confused into one.
Show answer · idempotency, outbox, contracts, reconciliation, time zones

I design for at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, because exactly-once across a queue, a database and an external system is a property nobody can actually promise. The message carries a stable business key — the record identity plus a version or sequence number — and the consumer writes into a table keyed by it, so a duplicate is a no-op instead of a double count. On the sending side, the outbox pattern: the transaction that changes state also writes the event to an outbox table in the same commit, and a dispatcher publishes it afterwards, so a crash between commit and publish cannot lose the event.

// Consumer side: the business key makes replay safe
public async Task HandleAsync(BookingEvent e, CancellationToken ct)
{
    if (await _store.ExistsAsync(e.BookingId, e.SequenceNumber, ct))
        return;                                  // duplicate: drop it, do not re-apply

    await _store.ApplyAsync(e, ct);               // same transaction as the version bump
}

Batch interfaces need the same rigour as APIs. A nightly schedule file (think IATA SSIM-style schedule data, or a fare file dropped over SFTP) is a full snapshot with an effective date, so the loader is a replace-with-reconciliation, not an append: validate the file against its expected record counts and date range, load in a staging area, diff against what is live, apply in one transaction, and write a reconciliation report an analyst can read. If the file is short by a hundred flights, the loader must fail — silently loading a partial schedule is how an optimizer ends up optimizing an airline that does not exist.

Contracts and change. Every inbound and outbound message is versioned, with additive changes only inside a major version; a new carrier-specific field does not break the consumer of the previous one. Where the counterparty can only do CSV, the validation is strict and the rejects are quarantined with the reason, not dropped.

Reconciliation closes the loop: a daily count of bookings received against bookings expected, per source and per day, with an alert on divergence. That single report catches most silent integration failures before a client does — a feed that stopped at 02:00 looks exactly like a quiet day otherwise.

Time. Everything is stored in UTC, with the local station time and the time zone of the airport or station kept as attributes, never inferred from the server. Departure dates are local business dates, which is precisely why daylight-saving transitions break naive implementations: a 02:30 local departure can exist twice or not at all. I make the time zone a column, test around the transition dates, and keep the conversion in one place in the codebase.

Strong signal: idempotency keyed by business identity, outbox for the write/publish gap, snapshot-with-reconciliation for file feeds, and time zone treated as data rather than as a formatting concern.
Likely follow-ups: What is your alert when a feed stops silently? · How do you cut over to a new interface without a freeze? · How do you replay six months of history safely? · Which of these failures would you expect an analyst to catch first?
Q5 Performance .NET batch · SQL

A network-wide optimization must finish before the planning window closes. Make the batch fast.

Question: “Picture a run over a full network — thousands of departures and tens of thousands of markets — that has to be published before the commercial day starts. It is taking too long. How do you find the time and where does it actually go?”
What is being probed: whether performance work is measurement or folklore. The strong answer knows that most of these runs are dominated by data movement and per-row logic, not by the solver, and that restartability is a performance feature.
Show answer · profiling first, set-based SQL, bounded parallelism, checkpointing

I instrument before I optimize. The run gets stage timings — extract, transform, forecast, optimize, publish — with row counts and elapsed time per stage, written to a run log. In my experience on data-heavy batches the split is usually unflattering to the code and not the algorithm: extraction with chatty ORM queries, per-row updates, and serial loops over markets dominate, while the solver is a fraction of the wall clock. That measurement is also what makes the conversation honest, because “the optimizer is slow” is a common and usually wrong diagnosis.

Then, in order of payoff:

  • Set-based SQL instead of per-row. Bulk insert with a table-valued parameter or SqlBulkCopy, one MERGE/UPDATE ... FROM instead of a thousand statements, and ExecuteUpdate/ExecuteDelete where EF Core would otherwise materialise entities one by one.
  • Stop paying for change tracking. AsNoTracking, projection straight to the shape the stage needs, and keyset pagination over large result sets so the process does not re-scan from the top.
  • Bounded parallelism, not maximum. Markets are independent for forecasting, so they parallelise well — with a concurrency limit tied to the database's ability to absorb it, because unrestricted Task.WhenAll over ten thousand items simply moves the bottleneck into connection-pool timeouts.
  • Incremental work. Only recompute markets whose inputs changed since the last run; a schedule change touching forty markets should not trigger a full recompute. Caching the expensive, stable parts (market definitions, fare structures) removes minutes with no loss of accuracy.
  • Restartability. Stage checkpoints so a failure at 80% resumes at 80%, not at zero. On a nightly window this is worth more than a micro-optimisation, because the second attempt is what turns a delay into a missed deadline.

I also fix the data path where the cheap wins are: correct indexes for the stage queries, no SELECT * into memory, streaming large extracts rather than buffering them, and Span/pooled buffers for the parsing loops that touch millions of rows. Then I guard the result with a performance test in CI over a representative data volume, so the next feature does not quietly give the time back.

Strong signal: per-stage measurement before tuning, set-based data access, bounded concurrency with the database as the constraint, incremental recompute, and checkpointing framed as part of the SLA.
Likely follow-ups: How do you decide the parallelism degree? · What would you cache and what must never be cached? · How do you test performance without a production-size database? · What is your rollback if the run completes but the controls look wrong?
Q6 Angular Planner UI · large grids

Analysts live in dense grids. Build an Angular screen that stays usable with tens of thousands of rows.

Question: “Our users are revenue analysts and planners, not consumers. They work all day in grids: schedules, fare overrides, protection levels, allotments. What do you do in Angular when the grid has tens of thousands of rows, several editable columns, filters and validation?”
What is being probed: whether the candidate has built a data-dense internal tool rather than a marketing page. The signals are server-side paging, virtual scrolling, change-detection discipline, and respect for keyboard workflows.
Show answer · virtual scroll, server-side data, editing, keyboard, exports

The rule is that the browser never holds ten thousand rows. Paging, filtering and sorting happen on the server; the grid asks for a window and renders a window. On the client, virtual scrolling (Angular CDK cdk-virtual-scroll-viewport, or the grid's own virtualisation) keeps the DOM at a few dozen rows regardless of the data set. That single decision removes most of the performance conversation.

Change detection comes next: OnPush everywhere, immutable updates, and signals for the local view state so a keystroke does not re-render the grid. Heavy derived work — formatting, grouping, client-side aggregations over the visible page — goes into computed() or a Web Worker when it is genuinely expensive, never into a getter called from the template.

Editing is where these screens fail. I use reactive forms with a FormArray only for the rows currently loaded, dirty-tracking per cell so only changed rows are sent, and optimistic save with an explicit conflict path (the row version is part of the payload; a 409 shows the other user's value instead of overwriting it). Unsaved-changes guards protect navigation, and validation errors are shown inline with the same validators used on the server, because the analyst must not discover them after a save.

// Only the edited rows leave the browser, with their version for optimistic concurrency
interface GridChange { id: string; version: number; field: string; value: unknown; }

save(changes: GridChange[]) {
  return this.api.patchProtections(changes).pipe(
    catchError(err => err.status === 409
      ? this.handleConflict(err.error)      // show both values, never silently overwrite
      : throwError(() => err))
  );
}

Then the things that make it a tool rather than a table: keyboard-first navigation with arrow keys, tab order, and copy/paste from Excel in both directions; column configuration saved per user; export of exactly what is filtered, not the whole table; a visible “as of” timestamp because the numbers come from last night's batch; and an accessible structure — real table semantics, focus management, and contrast that survives a beige office monitor.

Finally, feedback for slow operations: pessimistic actions get a progress state tied to the job, not a spinner on a button, and long-running server work returns a run id the UI polls or subscribes to.

Strong signal: server-side paging plus virtualisation as the first move, OnPush and signals for the rest, per-row dirty tracking with a conflict path, and keyboard/Excel workflows treated as requirements.
Likely follow-ups: How do you avoid a FormArray of ten thousand controls? · What would you move to a Web Worker? · How do you keep the grid in sync when the batch republishes? · What would you refuse to build in the browser?
Q7 Cargo Capacity · rating

Cargo does not sell seats. Model capacity and pricing when the unit is a kilogram.

Question: “You have worked on passenger problems. In cargo the unit of inventory is weight and volume, the products are shipments, and the commercial agreements are different. How would you model capacity, and what changes in the revenue logic?”
What is being probed: whether the candidate can transfer a model instead of repeating it. Cargo has dimensionality, allotments, routings and a rating engine — a candidate who maps seat inventory onto cargo one-to-one will get caught quickly.
Show answer · weight vs volume, ULD, allotments, rating, overbooking

The first difference is that capacity is two-dimensional: kilograms and cubic metres, constrained together, plus the physical envelope of the aircraft — positions for unit load devices on the main deck and the bulk hold. A shipment consumes whichever resource binds first, so the model has to carry both and check both, otherwise the system promises volume that does not fit or weight that cannot take off. Chargeable weight is the commercial unit: the greater of actual and volumetric weight, which is where the dimensional factor enters the pricing logic rather than the capacity logic.

The second difference is structure of demand. Passenger demand is per market and per departure; cargo demand arrives as a shipment with an air waybill, a commodity, pieces, dimensions, a requested routing and often a cut-off time. It can be split across flights, routed with transshipment, and moved between flights by operations, so the inventory model needs to reason about legs and connections the way an O&D model does — with the added complication that a shipment competes for space with others that may be re-routed at short notice.

The third is commercial commitment: allotments and block-space agreements reserve capacity for a customer whether or not it is used, and those obligations sit on top of free sale. Any availability logic has to respect the hierarchy — blocked space first, then contracts with priority rules, then free sale — because selling into capacity already committed is a contractual problem, not just a revenue one. Embargoes and dangerous-goods restrictions remove capacity for specific commodities and routes; no-shows behave differently from passengers and overbooking is a smaller, more negotiated lever.

Then rating: the price is built from a tariff and rules — minimum charges, weight breaks, pivot weights, surcharges, contract rates — and it must be deterministic, versioned and reproducible. When a customer disputes an invoice six weeks later, the system has to recompute the same number from the same inputs. That argues for a rating engine as a pure function over a versioned tariff, covered by golden-file tests, not for rate logic scattered through controllers.

What transfers from passenger revenue management is the discipline: forecast, allocate, price, measure. What does not transfer is the unit of inventory, the granularity of demand, and the weight of contractual commitments over pure optimisation.

Strong signal: weight and volume as simultaneous constraints, ULD positions, allotment hierarchy above free sale, and rating treated as a deterministic versioned engine.
Likely follow-ups: How would you allocate a shipment across two legs? · How do you price a shipment when the pivot weight changes? · What is the equivalent of a no-show in cargo? · How would you test a rating engine for regression?
Q8 Four verticals Ferry · Rail · Coach

One core, four industries. What changes when the same product serves an airline, a ferry operator and a rail company?

Question: “We sell into airline, cargo, cruise/ferry, rail and coach. If you were designing the product line, how would you keep one core instead of four forks — and what genuinely differs between the verticals?”
What is being probed: product architecture judgement. The weak answer is “we make it configurable”; the strong one names the specific dimension that varies, the abstraction that absorbs it, and what must not be abstracted.
Show answer · what varies per vertical, where to abstract, avoiding forks

What stays the same across all four is the loop: capacity, demand, forecast, control, price, measure. What changes is the definition of three things — the unit of capacity, the shape of the journey, and the sales channel. So I would model those three as first-class concepts with per-vertical implementations, and keep forecasting, overbooking, optimization and reporting on one shared core.

  • Airline. Unit is a seat in a cabin; the journey is O&D over legs with connections; multiple fare classes with nested availability; the highest-frequency, highest-volume case and the one that pushes performance hardest.
  • Ferry and cruise. Capacity is composite: passenger seats or berths, cabin grades, and on many routes vehicle deck space (car, coach, freight), and the passenger and vehicle are coupled — a booking consumes both, and deck space is measured in lane metres, not units. Journeys are crossings with a departure time and a return leg that is often priced jointly; seasonality is extreme and the leisure share dominates, so price elasticity matters more than fare fences.
  • Rail. Capacity is per segment and often per seat with reservations on top, so inventory has to reason over segment legs rather than whole trips; a passenger occupying a seat from A to C blocks B to C as well. Connections, rail passes and operator-specific reservation rules add constraints that do not exist in the air model.
  • Coach. The simplest: a vehicle with seats, a timetable, mostly leg-based inventory, less fare complexity — the case where a heavy network optimizer is over-engineering and a clean allocation model wins.

Architecturally, that means the capacity model is an interface with per-vertical implementations over a shared inventory core; the fare and rule evaluation is a shared engine parameterised by vertical; and the reporting layer maps to shared KPI definitions (yield, load factor, revenue per available unit) even where the unit differs. Configuration handles the data, not the logic — a vertical that needs different behaviour gets an implementation, not fifty flags.

And the discipline that keeps it one product: no client-specific branching in shared code. Carrier or operator specifics live in configuration, rule sets or a plugin boundary with its own tests, because the moment a fork exists, every future fix has to be made four times and one of them will be forgotten.

Strong signal: the three named dimensions (unit of capacity, journey shape, channel), concrete ferry and rail details such as vehicle deck coupling and segment-leg inventory, and a firm rule against client-specific branches in the core.
Likely follow-ups: Where would you draw the plugin boundary? · How do you keep KPI definitions comparable across verticals? · What would you refuse to make configurable? · Which vertical would you onboard second and why?
Q9 Modernization Legacy · .NET 8 · Angular

A product from 1991 with decades of domain logic in it. Modernize it without a rewrite.

Question: “Some of what we maintain has been in production for a very long time, and the domain rules inside it are the company's real asset — some of them exist only as behaviour. If you were asked to move it to .NET 8 and Angular, how would you do it without betting the business on a big-bang rewrite?”
What is being probed: whether the candidate understands that the risk is losing behaviour, not losing code. Characterization tests, seams and parallel running are the vocabulary of someone who has done this rather than read about it.
Show answer · strangler fig, characterization tests, golden data, expand/contract

I treat the existing system as the specification. Before moving anything I build characterization tests: capture real inputs and the outputs the business has accepted, freeze them as golden files, and run any new implementation against them. That converts “the rules exist only as behaviour” into an executable contract — and it is the only way to prove equivalence when nobody can fully articulate the rule, which is the normal state of a thirty-year-old pricing or allocation engine.

Then the strangler fig: pick a seam at the edge of the domain — a report, a read-only screen, one market type — and route that path through the new implementation while the old one keeps serving everything else. Coexistence is explicit: an anti-corruption layer translates between the two models so the legacy schema does not leak into the new code, and a routing switch selects old or new per tenant or per capability, not per deployment.

  • Parallel run before cutover: both paths compute, the new one is compared against the old one in production for a defined period, and differences are triaged — a genuine bug, a rounding difference, or a rule nobody documented.
  • Database by expand and contract: add the new column or table, dual-write, backfill, read from the new path, then remove the old one in a later release. Never a destructive migration in the same step as a deploy.
  • API surface versioned from the first release, so clients of the old interface are not forced to move in step with the internal refactor.
  • Deterministic cores first: rating, proration and allocation logic are the highest-value and best-testable targets, because they are pure functions over data and their equivalence can be proven with golden files.

What I would explicitly not do: rewrite the whole thing in parallel with a feature freeze, or start from the UI. A new Angular front end over the same tangled services usually delivers a modern look with the same defect rate, and it removes the pressure that makes the backend work possible.

Success is measured by defect rate, lead time for a rule change and the share of traffic on the new path — not by lines of code migrated. And each step has to be reversible: if the switch can only be turned on, it is not a controlled migration.

Strong signal: golden-file characterization of undocumented rules, routing by capability with parallel run, expand/contract migrations, and a refusal to start from the UI.
Likely follow-ups: How do you get golden data out of a system nobody fully understands? · When do you delete the legacy path? · How do you keep two codebases from diverging for a year? · What would make you stop the migration?
Q10 Delivery Distributed teams · support

Plano, the UK, South Africa and India — plus airline clients in peak season. How do you work and how do you support?

Question: “The team is spread across Dallas, the UK, South Africa and India, and our clients operate 24/7 with seasonal peaks. How do you work effectively in that setup, and what do you do when something breaks during an airline's busiest week?”
What is being probed: asynchronous communication discipline and production maturity. In a distributed team, the written artefact is the coordination mechanism; in a 24/7 operation, the runbook and the rollback path are the product.
Show answer · async by default, decision records, severity model, peak freezes

I assume overlap is scarce and make the written record carry the work: a short spec or ticket with the acceptance criteria and the data shape before coding, decisions as one-page records (context, options, decision, consequence), and pull requests sized so a reviewer in another time zone can finish them in one sitting. Anything urgent gets a synchronous call, but the outcome goes back into the written record — a decision that exists only in a call is lost by the next handover.

For a client-facing role I also keep the escalation path explicit: who talks to the client, which channel, and in what language. With US-based stakeholders and B2+ English expected, I would rather say “I don't know yet, I will confirm by 16:00 your time” than improvise an answer that has to be retracted.

On support, I work from a severity definition everyone agrees on, not from whoever shouts loudest:

  • Sev 1 — revenue decisions wrong or unavailable in a peak window: mitigate first (rollback, fall back to the last published controls, disable the feature flag), communicate a status on a fixed cadence, then diagnose.
  • Sev 2 — degraded: a client affected but able to operate; workaround documented while the fix is developed.
  • Sev 3 — non-urgent defect, planned into the next release.

Every Sev 1 gets a runbook entry before the postmortem: symptom, what to check in what order, the exact rollback step, who to call, and the client-facing message. Data corrections are treated as a separate risk class — a script that fixes rows in a client's production database runs with a reviewed statement, a transaction, a before/after count, and a full record of what changed, because an unaudited data fix is indistinguishable from corruption afterwards.

Finally, the calendar is part of the engineering plan. Airlines and ferry operators run on seasonal schedules and peak demand periods, so releases respect change freezes around schedule changeover and holidays, and anything risky ships with enough time to observe it before the peak rather than during it. That is a conversation to have at planning time, not at 23:00 on a Friday.

Strong signal: written artefacts as the coordination mechanism across time zones, an agreed severity model with mitigation before diagnosis, runbooks and audited data fixes, and release timing tied to the client's commercial calendar.
Likely follow-ups: How do you hand over an incident to another region? · What would you automate first in the support path? · How do you push back on a release during a freeze? · What do you document after a Sev 2?
