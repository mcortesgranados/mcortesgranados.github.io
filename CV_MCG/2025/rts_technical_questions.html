Interview Questions — Solving RTS Business Problems

Twenty questions written for the person who will actually be in the room: a cargo technology executive with two decades inside RTS, not a front-end specialist. The emphasis is on how software changes a client's margin, how an analyst's problem becomes a product that works for several carriers, and how risk is managed when the customer operates 24/7. Each answer is meant to be spoken in about a minute, with one sentence of technical proof where it adds credibility. Part 1 covers the business conversation; part 2 takes each line of the posted job description and turns it into a client-facing problem with a solution and a way to measure it.

Q1 Opening Motivation

Tell me about yourself — and why cargo revenue management?

Question: “Give me a short introduction, and tell me why you are interested in this role and in our cargo business.”
What he is really testing: whether you can summarise twenty-eight years in ninety seconds without listing technologies, and whether the interest in cargo is thought through rather than simply available enthusiasm.
Show answer · 90-second structure, the cargo hook, what to leave out

Three movements, then stop: what I do, the problem I solve repeatedly, and why this business.

What I do. “I am a software engineer with 28 years in enterprise systems — mostly .NET back ends, Angular front ends, integrations and data — always on systems where the numbers have to be right: financial calculations, regulatory reporting, operational planning.”

The problem I solve well. “My strongest pattern is taking a process a business team runs on spreadsheets and tribal knowledge, and turning it into a system they trust — rules explicit, data reconciled, decisions auditable. That is the work I enjoy, because the value shows up in the client's numbers, not in a demo.”

Why cargo. “Cargo is the version of revenue management where the product is not homogeneous: weight, volume, routing, allotments and rating all interact, and a mistake costs margin or breaks a contract. It is also a product line with decades of established behaviour, so I would rather learn it from people who know it than work on something generic.”

Say it like this: “I turn processes that live in spreadsheets into systems a business team trusts, and cargo is where that skill has the most direct effect on margin.”
One line of proof, if asked: “The stack is familiar — .NET 8 with layered services, Angular for planner screens, SQL Server with EF Core or Dapper — so my learning curve is the domain, not the technology.”
If he pushes: Which part of your experience transfers fastest? · What do you want to learn in the first six months? · Have you worked with transport or logistics clients before?
Q2 Cargo revenue management Plain language

What does revenue management mean in cargo? Explain it as you would to a client.

Question: “You would be working on cargo revenue management products. Tell me what you understand by that, in your own words — no need for formulas.”
What he is really testing: whether you reach for the business meaning or recite textbook jargon, and whether you know cargo is not passenger seats: the unit is a kilogram and a cubic metre, and the customer is often a forwarder with a contract.
Show answer · capacity, price per kg, commitments, the two expensive mistakes

“Every flight that departs has capacity that disappears the moment the door closes — and it is sold in kilograms and cubic metres, not seats. Revenue management is how we decide who gets that space, at what price, and how much we hold back for better-paying demand that may still arrive.”

Three things make cargo different, and I would say them explicitly:

  • Capacity is two-dimensional. A shipment consumes weight and volume at the same time, sometimes a specific container position; whichever runs out first is the real constraint. So “we have space” is never a single number.
  • Much of the space is already promised. Allotments and block-space agreements reserve capacity whether the customer uses it or not, so the commercial question is not only “what price” but “what did we commit to, and what is genuinely free to sell”.
  • The product has a route and a deadline. A shipment can be split, re-routed or lost to a competitor because it missed a cut-off. Availability and pricing have to respect the physical journey, not just the leg.

Then the value: the software exists to reduce the two expensive mistakes — selling capacity too cheaply too early, and departing with space we could have sold. When that trade-off is visible, repeatable and defensible to a client, the system has done its job.

Say it like this: “Cargo revenue management is deciding who gets space that expires, when it is measured in kilograms and cubic metres and much of it is already committed by contract — and the software makes that decision repeatable instead of personal.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Technically that means a capacity model carrying weight, volume and ULD positions together, and a rating engine deterministic enough to reproduce an invoice six weeks later.”
If he pushes: What would you look at first in a cargo P&L? · What is hardest about cargo forecasting, in your view? · Where does cargo lag passenger revenue management?
Q3 Diagnosis Client outcome

A client says volumes are growing but revenue is flat. How do you investigate?

Question: “One of our cargo customers tells us volumes are up but revenue has not moved. They are pressing us to ‘improve the system’. Where do you start?”
What he is really testing: whether you jump to a technical answer or first establish what the business is seeing. A VP wants someone who protects the client from a wrong fix as much as someone who ships.
Show answer · hypothesis tree, mix versus yield, what to ask, how to prove it

“I would resist changing the system until we know which story is true, because the fix is different in each case.”

The decomposition I would put on a whiteboard, in business language:

  • Mix. Are the extra kilograms low-yield traffic — cheap commodities, promotional rates, contract renewals at lower rates? Volume up with revenue flat is often mix, not system failure.
  • Yield per kilogram by lane and account. If one large forwarder renegotiated, the aggregate hides it. Split revenue by route, commodity and customer before touching a line of code.
  • Utilisation and allocation. Are we flying fuller but giving away space we should have protected? Are allotments under-used while free sale is closed?
  • Data quality. The last possibility, and the one that wastes the most time when ignored: are we measuring the same thing? Missing shipments, duplicated AWBs, cut-off timing or currency would produce exactly this symptom.

My next step is a working session with their commercial team plus one data extract — a month of shipments and the last two rate reviews — and come back with three views: revenue per kilogram by lane, utilisation versus allotment, and top accounts year on year. Usually the conversation changes there. Sometimes the request becomes “help us see this monthly”, which is a cheaper and more valuable deliverable than a new optimizer.

Say it like this: “Before changing the system I would separate mix from yield from allocation using their own data, because two of those three are commercial decisions and only one is ours to fix in software.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That starts as a reconciliation: shipment counts and revenue by lane, so we know the numbers agree before drawing conclusions from them.”
If he pushes: What if the data cannot be reconciled quickly? · How do you tell a client the problem is their pricing? · What would you deliver in the first two weeks?
Q4 Requirements Users

Our users are analysts, not engineers. How do you get from their complaint to something we can build?

Question: “Our revenue analysts and planners know the business but they do not write specifications. How do you turn what they tell you into software that actually helps them?”
What he is really testing: whether you have sat next to a real user. The jump from “they asked for a button” to “here is the decision they are making and the data it needs” is what separates a developer from someone who can own a client relationship.
Show answer · observe the decision, prototype with real data, acceptance in business terms

“I start by watching the work, not by asking for requirements. The request people write down is usually their proposed solution; the useful information is in the spreadsheet they keep next to it, because that is the tool they actually trust.”

Concretely, four steps:

  • Observe one real cycle. Sit with an analyst through a full run — the report they open, the exceptions they chase, the call they make at the end. I write down the decision, the inputs it depends on, and what they do when a number looks wrong.
  • Take their artefact seriously. If they export to Excel and add three columns, those three columns are the requirement. Reproducing them in the product is usually the fastest path to trust.
  • Prototype on their data. One screen with real numbers and a “does this match what you expect?” conversation beats a document. Disagreements surface in minutes, and the analyst becomes a co-designer.
  • Agree the acceptance in business terms. “The morning check takes ten minutes instead of two hours, and the exceptions list matches the one you build manually” — not “the grid supports filtering”.

I also make the trade-off explicit early: what we are not doing in this version, and what they will keep doing in Excel for now. That single sentence prevents most of the disappointment later, and it is usually the thing the client remembers you for.

Say it like this: “I watch one real cycle, take their spreadsheet as the specification, prototype on their own data, and agree the acceptance in the language of their morning routine.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Angular-side that usually means server-side paging and a grid built for keyboard work, plus an export that matches what they already send to their commercial team.”
If he pushes: What do you do when two clients want contradictory behaviour? · How do you handle a user who cannot articulate the rule? · How much of this can be done remotely?
Q5 Product judgement Customization

One carrier wants something that could break the product for everyone else.

Question: “A large customer asks for a change that makes sense for them but would complicate the product for the rest of the clients. They are important. What do you do?”
What he is really testing: whether you can hold a product line together while keeping a key account. In a company of this size, one unmanaged customization can cost years of maintenance — and one clumsy refusal can cost a client.
Show answer · need versus solution, configuration versus fork, how to say no

“First I separate the need from the requested solution, because the request is usually one idea among several that would solve the same problem.”

Then a short decision path I would take to the product owner, not decide alone:

  • Is the underlying need shared? If two or three other carriers have the same pain, this is a product feature with a general design, and the requesting client becomes the pilot.
  • Can it be configuration? Rates, rules, thresholds and workflows are legitimate places for per-client variation. If the difference is data and policy rather than logic, configuration is the cheap answer — as long as it is designed once and tested.
  • If it is truly unique, it is a funded extension. Code paths per client are a maintenance tax that outlives the deal: named owner, tests, documentation, and a conversation about who pays for it and for how long.
  • Say no with an alternative. “We will not branch the core, but here is what we can do in this release, and here is the workaround for the next two quarters.” A no with a date is a plan; a no without one is a rejection.

The part I would not skip is writing the decision down — what was asked, what we chose, what we gave up — because in two years someone will ask why the product behaves this way, and the answer should not depend on memory.

Say it like this: “I separate the need from the requested solution, look for configuration before code, and if it is genuinely unique I make it a funded extension with an owner — and I say no with a date and an alternative, never a bare no.”
One line of proof, if asked: “The technical version of that rule is: client-specific variation lives in configuration or a plugin boundary with its own tests, never in branches inside shared code.”
If he pushes: How do you handle the sales pressure? · Who should own that decision? · Have you ever removed a customization?
Q6 Data trust Client confidence

Airline data arrives late, incomplete or duplicated. How do you protect the revenue decision?

Question: “Our data comes from schedules, reservation systems, fare files and revenue accounting — and some of it is late, missing or inconsistent. How do you stop that from producing bad decisions or, worse, bad advice to the client?”
What he is really testing: whether you understand that the danger is not a crashed job but a successful run on wrong data. That distinction is the whole conversation.
Show answer · fail loudly, reconcile, show freshness, alert the client

“The dangerous case is not a failure — it is a run that completes with incomplete data and publishes numbers nobody questions. So my first rule is that missing input stops the decision, rather than becoming a zero.”

In practice, four habits:

  • Validate at the door. Expected record counts, date ranges and mandatory fields. A schedule file short by a hundred flights should reject, not load partially — a partial schedule produces a confident, wrong plan.
  • Reconcile, then publish. Compare what arrived against what was expected, per source and per day, and write that report where a human can see it. A feed that stopped at two in the morning looks exactly like a quiet day unless someone counts.
  • Show freshness in the product. The screen should say “as of last night's cycle”, and where a figure is estimated or carried forward, say so. Analysts forgive a known gap; they do not forgive a silent one.
  • Tell the client early. If a decision is based on incomplete data, that is a conversation with the account team the same day, not a footnote in a release note.

Where a rule must tolerate missing data — a new route with no history, a capacity record not yet loaded — I prefer an explicit business default that is visible and reviewable over a silent fallback. And after every incident of this kind, the fix is a check plus an alert, not “we will be more careful”.

Say it like this: “Bad data is more dangerous when the job succeeds than when it fails, so missing input stops the decision, and the count is always reconciled before anything is published.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That is idempotent loading with a business key, a staging and quarantine path, and a daily reconciliation report with an alert on divergence.”
If he pushes: What do you do when the client's own feed is wrong? · How do you handle a time-zone or cut-off ambiguity? · Which of these would you automate first?
Q7 Modernization Business risk

Some of our products have been in production since the nineties. How would you modernize one without risking a client's operation?

Question: “We have long-lived products in production for major carriers. If you were asked to modernize one, how would you do it without putting a client's operation at risk?”
What he is really testing: whether you think in slices and reversibility, or in rewrites. With twenty years of client history behind the product, the fear is not the technology — it is losing behaviour nobody documented.
Show answer · small slices, pilot client, parallel run, reversibility, visible value

“I would treat the running system as the specification and never take a big step without a way back. The risk is not that the new code is worse — it is that a rule which only exists as behaviour gets quietly lost.”

How that looks in practice:

  • Freeze the behaviour first. Capture real inputs and the outputs the client accepted, and keep them as a comparison set. Any new version has to reproduce them before it is allowed near a client.
  • One slice at a time. Start where the value is visible and the risk is low — a report, one screen, one market type — rather than at the database or the framework.
  • Pilot with one willing client. Run both paths in parallel, compare the numbers daily, and let the client see the comparison. Their confidence is the real acceptance test.
  • Keep the switch reversible. Per client and per capability, so a problem is a switch back, not an incident call at midnight. A migration you can only move forwards is not controlled.
  • Respect their calendar. No cutover near a schedule change or a peak season; the client's commercial year sets the release dates, not ours.

And I would be honest about pace: this is a programme measured in quarters, with each step worth something on its own. If the first release is only infrastructure, the client loses interest and the funding disappears before the value arrives.

Say it like this: “Treat the live system as the specification, move one slice at a time with a pilot client, compare the numbers in parallel, and keep every step reversible — the client's calendar sets the pace.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That is a strangler approach with an adapter in front of the old schema, database changes done as add, backfill and remove in separate releases, and API versions so clients do not move in lockstep.”
If he pushes: How do you get the comparison data out of a system nobody fully understands? · When do you retire the old path? · What would make you stop the programme?
Q8 Support Client trust

Something breaks during a client's peak week. What do you do, in what order?

Question: “A carrier calls on a Monday morning in their busiest week: the numbers on the planning screen are wrong and they have decisions to make today. Walk me through your first hour.”
What he is really testing: whether you protect the client before you satisfy your own curiosity, and whether you communicate while you work. In a company this size, the second is often what the client remembers.
Show answer · stop the bleeding, communicate, restore, then fix and prevent

“First hour, in this order: stop the damage, tell them what we know, restore a trustworthy number, and only then investigate.”

  • Stop the bleeding. If the last published decision is still valid, revert to it or switch the affected capability off. A client can work with yesterday's numbers; they cannot work with numbers they do not trust.
  • Communicate with a named owner and a cadence. One person on our side owns the client conversation, with an update every thirty minutes even when there is nothing new. Silence is what turns a technical incident into a relationship problem.
  • Restore, then diagnose. Confirm the data path first — a feed that arrived late produces the same symptom as a logic bug — and be explicit about which figures are safe to use.
  • Fix the data, carefully. Any correction in the client's production data runs with a reviewed statement, a before-and-after count and a record of what changed. An unaudited data fix is indistinguishable from corruption afterwards.
  • Prevent it visibly. A monitoring check plus an alert before the client notices, and a short written review of what happened and what changed — no blame, just the missing check.

The judgement I would show is about expectations: if the root cause will take two days, say two days on the first call rather than a hopeful “soon”. Clients forgive a hard truth early far more easily than a soft promise that slips.

Say it like this: “Revert to a number they can trust, put one named owner on the client with updates every half hour, then diagnose — and any data correction comes with a count and a record.”
One line of proof, if asked: “That requires severity levels agreed in advance and a runbook per product — symptom, checks in order, rollback step, who to call — written before the peak, not during it.”
If he pushes: What if the client's team caused it? · How do you keep the account team informed? · What do you change afterwards so it does not repeat?
Q9 AI Risk and value

Where does AI genuinely help this business — and where does it create risk?

Question: “Everyone is being asked about AI. In our context — airline and cargo clients, pricing logic, sensitive commercial data — where do you see real value, and what would you refuse to do?”
What he is really testing: whether you can separate useful productivity from a demo. A credible answer names a concrete task, a limit, and who approves the output.
Show answer · concrete uses, hard limits, governance in one breath

“Value first, and it is mostly in time saved around the work rather than in the decision itself.”

Where I have seen it genuinely help:

  • Reading and summarising. Logs, incident timelines, long requirement threads and legacy documentation — a first draft in seconds that a person then corrects.
  • Test and query drafting. Generating test cases for edge conditions I might forget, or a first version of a reporting query that an analyst then reviews against the real schema.
  • Explaining code and rules. Making an unfamiliar module or an undocumented calculation readable, which is exactly the bottleneck in long-lived products.
  • Support triage. Classifying incoming tickets and drafting the first reply, always with a human sending it.

What I would refuse or restrict: client commercial data in public tools without an approved path; generated pricing or allocation logic promoted without validation against known outcomes; and anything that writes to a production database on its own. In this domain a plausible-looking rule is more dangerous than an obvious error, because it survives review.

The governance I would propose is simple enough to actually be followed: approved tools only, no client identifiers leaving the environment, generated code reviewed like anyone else's, and a named person accountable for every output that reaches a client. Then measure whether it saved time — if it does not show up in cycle time or defect rate, it is entertainment.

Say it like this: “It buys time on reading, drafting and explaining — not on deciding. Nothing generated touches a client's pricing logic or data without a named person validating it.”
One line of proof, if asked: “I use it daily for tests, refactors and reading unfamiliar code, and the rule I apply to my own work is that a generated change is reviewed line by line and covered by a test that fails without it.”
If he pushes: Have you seen it produce something wrong that passed review? · How would you introduce it to a sceptical team? · Where would AI help our analysts specifically?
Q10 Ways of working Distributed team

Dallas, the UK, South Africa, India — and clients in other time zones. How do you keep a team aligned?

Question: “Our teams and clients are spread across several countries. How do you work in that setup without things falling between the cracks?”
What he is really testing: whether you have actually worked distributed, and whether you write things down. In a cargo VP's world, the cost of an unclear decision is a client escalation three weeks later.
Show answer · written by default, one owner, overlap, mentoring, client language

“I assume overlap is scarce and make the written record carry the coordination.”

  • Decisions written in one page. Context, options, what we chose, what we gave up. Anything agreed on a call goes back into writing the same day, because a decision that lives only in a call disappears at the next handover.
  • One owner per topic. Not a committee. The owner is whoever can answer “why is it like this?” six months later, and they are named in the ticket.
  • Small, reviewable pieces. Work sized so a colleague in another time zone can review it in one sitting; a pull request nobody can finish before their evening is a blocked day, not a code review.
  • Protect the overlap hour. Use it for the conversations that need nuance — a client issue, a design argument, feedback — and keep status out of meetings entirely.
  • Mentoring by default. In distributed teams juniors get less accidental learning, so it has to be deliberate: pairing sessions, review comments that explain the why, and a named person for each new joiner.

With clients, the same discipline plus one extra rule: say what you know, say what you do not, and give a time when you will. “I do not know yet, I will confirm by four your time” protects credibility far better than a fast guess — and in an English-speaking client relationship, clarity matters more than perfect grammar.

Say it like this: “Written by default, one named owner per decision, small reviewable pieces, and the overlap hour reserved for things that need a conversation.”
One line of proof, if asked: “In practice that is trunk-based work with short branches, pipeline checks that cannot be skipped, and the reason for each change captured in the pull request.”
If he pushes: How do you handle a disagreement across regions? · What do you do when a client wants a daily call? · How do you onboard someone remotely?

Q11 JD: .NET 8+ / Angular 13+ Platform upgrade

“Develop and maintain enterprise applications using .NET 8+ and Angular 13+.” Make that a business case, not a version number.

Question: “The posting says .NET 8 and Angular 13 or later. A client does not care about the version. How would you explain to them — or to me — why moving is worth the money?”
What he is really testing: whether you can attach a platform decision to risk and velocity. He has watched upgrades consume a year; the answer he wants includes what happens if we do not move.
Show answer · risk, velocity, funding model, how to prove it

“I would never sell it as a version upgrade. I would sell it as two business risks and one number.”

  • The risk of standing still. Unsupported runtimes stop receiving security fixes, and in a system that holds a carrier's commercial data that becomes an audit finding, not an engineering preference. It also gets harder to hire for, and every integration the client asks for next year costs more than it should.
  • The velocity cost. Old platforms make small changes slow, so the client waits longer for the requests they actually care about. That is the argument that gets budget: not “we are on an old framework”, but “each change costs us twice what it should”.
  • The funding model. A separate modernization project competes with features and loses. I would ring-fence a slice of capacity — around a fifth of the team — and attach a visible client benefit to each slice: one report, one screen, one integration. Funded from the roadmap, not from a hope.

The sequence that usually works is to separate the front end from the services first, because the UI delivers visible value quickly and the back end can move underneath it without changing what the client sees. Then each release modernizes one path end to end instead of half of everything.

And I would commit to numbers before starting: incidents per quarter, average time to deliver a client change, and support hours per client. If those three do not improve, the programme is not working, whatever the version says.

Say it like this: “It is not a version upgrade — it is a security and audit risk on one side, and the cost of every future client change on the other. I would fund it as a slice of roadmap capacity and prove it with lead time and incidents.”
One line of proof, if asked: “In practice: upgrade the services behind the existing contracts, keep API versions so clients do not move in lockstep, and ship the front end in routes rather than as a big-bang replacement.”
If he pushes: What if the client refuses to pay? · How do you keep features shipping meanwhile? · What would make you stop and re-plan?
Q12 JD: responsive UI, CSS3/SCSS Adoption

“Build responsive web interfaces.” Where does responsiveness actually pay for itself in a cargo operation?

Question: “Responsive design sounds like a design concern. In our world, where does it change a client's operation — or their cost?”
What he is really testing: whether you see the two different workplaces behind the request. Analysts work in dense grids on large screens; acceptance staff work standing up, on a tablet, with gloves on.
Show answer · two work modes, error cost, what to measure

“Responsiveness is an adoption question before it is a CSS question. In cargo there are two workplaces, and they need opposite things.”

  • Desk mode. Revenue analysts and planners in front of large screens, comparing many rows. Here responsiveness means density and control — more information per screen, keyboard navigation, no wasted whitespace.
  • Field mode. Cargo acceptance and warehouse staff on a tablet or handheld, entering weight and dimensions next to the shipment. Here it means large targets, few fields per step, and tolerance for an interrupted connection.

Both come from one codebase with a shared style layer — tokens for spacing and type, breakpoints that switch layout rather than shrink it, and components that declare their density. The trap is “make it smaller”: a dense grid squeezed onto a tablet is unusable, and a warehouse form stretched onto a desktop wastes the screen.

The business case is in errors and time. A weight entered on the wrong shipment costs a re-rating and a customer complaint; a screen that takes six taps instead of two costs minutes per shipment across a shift. So I would measure entries per hour, correction rate and support calls about the screen — and I would sit with acceptance staff before designing anything, because their constraints are physical, not aesthetic.

Say it like this: “Two workplaces, two designs: density for the analyst at a large screen, and a task-by-task flow with big targets for acceptance staff on a tablet — measured in entries per hour and correction rate.”
One line of proof, if asked: “One codebase, one SCSS token layer, breakpoints that change the layout instead of shrinking it, and components that declare their own density.”
If he pushes: Would you build a separate mobile app? · How do you test this without field access? · What is the first screen you would fix?
Q13 JD: Angular from scratch New product

“Angular applications from scratch, including initial architecture.” A new product line, a small team, a fixed budget — how do you scope it?

Question: “Suppose we want a first product for coach operators — a market we serve but where the needs are simpler. Small team, fixed budget, one year. How do you decide what exists at the end?”
What he is really testing: whether you scope by business decisions rather than by feature list, and whether you can say what will deliberately not be built.
Show answer · minimum viable value, capability modules, pilot operator, what to defer

“With a fixed budget I would fix the scope, never the date, and I would define scope by the decisions the operator needs to make — not by screens.”

For a coach operator the first release probably needs three decisions to be supported: what to charge for a departure, how much capacity to release at each price, and how the week actually performed. That is a fares screen with guardrails, a simple allocation view, and one report. Everything else — optimizers, complex fare rules, multi-currency, deep integrations — waits for evidence that someone is using the first three.

  • One pilot operator with a named counterpart. Their real timetable and fares as the only data set for the pilot. It keeps the scope honest and gives a reference client.
  • Capability per module. Angular set up with one lazily loaded area per capability, so the second and third releases add routes instead of restructuring.
  • Configuration over cleverness. Coach is the simplest vertical; the product should win by being quick to configure per operator, not by matching airline sophistication.
  • A visible first release in eight to twelve weeks. Something the operator uses daily, even if narrow. A year-long build with nothing to show loses its sponsor before it ships.

The number that matters at the end is not features delivered but whether the pilot operator runs their week in the tool. If they still keep the spreadsheet alongside it, the scope was wrong regardless of what was built.

Say it like this: “With a fixed budget I fix scope by the decisions the operator must make, ship something usable in eight to twelve weeks, and let the pilot's usage — not a feature list — decide release two.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Angular from scratch with one lazy area per capability, a strict TypeScript baseline from day one, and configuration per operator instead of branches per client.”
If he pushes: What would you cut first if the budget shrinks? · How do you avoid rebuilding the airline product? · Who owns the roadmap decisions?
Q14 JD: reusable components, modules, services, directives, pipes Cost of delivery

“Build reusable components, modules, services, directives and pipes.” Four verticals need the same screen — how does that save money?

Question: “Airline, cargo, ferry and rail all need a rate screen, but not the same one. How do you build it once without it becoming a compromise nobody likes?”
What he is really testing: whether you can reuse without over-abstracting, and whether you can express the saving in days or in euros rather than in principles.
Show answer · shared core plus per-vertical configuration, the three-use rule, the saving

“The saving is real but it only appears if the shared part is the boring part. What differs between verticals is the unit — a seat, a kilogram, a lane metre, a berth — and the vocabulary around it.”

So I split the screen in two layers:

  • Shared: the table mechanics — paging, filtering, editing, validation display, keyboard navigation, export, audit of changes. None of that is vertical-specific, and it is where most of the effort goes.
  • Per vertical: a configuration of columns, units, labels and rules, plus at most one custom cell component where the domain genuinely differs. Configuration, not a fork.

My working rule against over-abstraction is three uses: the second time I copy something I leave it alone, the third time I extract it. Extracting on the first sighting produces abstractions shaped by one carrier's exception, and those cost more than the duplication they replaced.

The business argument is simple arithmetic: if a screen costs three weeks the first time, the second and third vertical should cost days, not weeks. I would track days per new screen per vertical as the measure — if it stays flat, the reuse is theoretical. And the practical side benefit is consistency: an analyst moving from an airline product to a cargo one recognizes the same interactions, which shortens training on every new client.

Say it like this: “Share the mechanics, configure the meaning. The first vertical pays for the screen, the next ones should cost days — and I would track days per screen to prove it.”
One line of proof, if asked: “A shared component library with per-vertical configuration, directives and pipes for the formatting rules, and the three-use rule to keep the abstraction honest.”
If he pushes: When would you accept a copy instead of a component? · How do you version the library across products? · What if one vertical needs a completely different interaction?
Q15 JD: lazy loading, guards, interceptors, RxJS, state, reactive forms Three client complaints

“Lazy loading, route guards, interceptors, RxJS, state management, reactive forms.” Which client complaints do those six items actually fix?

Question: “Those are the buzzwords in the posting. Translate them for me: what does a client stop complaining about when they are done well?”
What he is really testing: whether you can map technical mechanisms to symptoms a client reports. This is the question where a candidate either sounds like a framework manual or like someone who has run support.
Show answer · slow open, wrong visibility, lost edits — and the measure for each

“Three complaints, in the order clients usually raise them.”

  • “It takes forever to open.” Lazy loading and a smaller first bundle. The analyst logs in to check one market before a meeting; if the whole application loads first, those minutes are the product's fault. Measure: time to the first useful screen.
  • “I can see another carrier's rates.” Route guards plus authorization on the server — the guard hides the screen, the API refuses the data. In a multi-tenant product this is not a UI nicety; it is commercial confidentiality, and a single incident costs a client relationship. Interceptors carry the identity and the correlation id so an incident can be traced. Measure: entitlement defects, which should be zero.
  • “I entered it twice and it disappeared.” State management and reactive forms with dirty tracking, so a refresh or a timeout does not silently discard work, and a second user's change is shown as a conflict instead of being overwritten. Measure: rework tickets and repeated entries.

RxJS is the connective tissue in all three: cancellation so a stale response cannot overwrite a fresh one, debouncing so typing does not flood the API, and a single place to handle a session expiry instead of every screen inventing its own behaviour. The technical choices are ordinary; the point is that each one has a symptom attached, and I would only adopt the heavier ones — a store, for instance — when a symptom justifies it.

Say it like this: “Slow open, wrong visibility, lost edits — lazy loading, guards with server-side authorization, and dirty tracking with conflict handling. Each has a number attached, and none of them is worth doing without the symptom.”
One line of proof, if asked: “In Angular that is lazy routes per feature, a guard for navigation and a policy on the API, an HTTP interceptor for identity and correlation, and RxJS with switchMap so obsolete responses are cancelled.”
If he pushes: Where would you not use a full state store? · How do you prove a tenant can never see another's data? · What is your first fix on a slow screen?
Q16 JD: REST APIs, third-party integrations Partner channel

“Design and implement RESTful APIs and third-party integrations.” A carrier wants our rates inside their own portal. What do you deliver, and what do you refuse?

Question: “A good customer asks us to expose rates and availability so their own sales portal can quote directly. Commercially it is attractive. How would you scope it?”
What he is really testing: whether you can say yes to the commercial intent while protecting the product — and whether you think about load, support and the contract before the endpoint.
Show answer · read versus write, contract, limits, support model

“I would say yes to the intent and be precise about the boundary: we publish what they need to quote, and we do not let an external system drive pricing.”

  • Read, not write. Rates and availability out; no external writes into pricing rules or capacity. The moment a partner can change a fare through an API, every dispute becomes our problem.
  • A contract before code. Agreed fields, refresh expectations, error behaviour and versioning. A documented contract is what makes an integration supportable two years later, when the person who built it has moved on.
  • Commercial limits. Rate limits, quotas and caching expectations, written into the agreement. A portal that polls every second is a capacity problem for every other client on the platform.
  • Identity per client. Their own credentials, scoped to their data only, with logging of what was requested — that is also what protects us in a billing or entitlement discussion.

The business upside is concrete: fewer manual quote requests by email, faster response on spot business, and a switching cost for the carrier because our data is already inside their tool. The measure is manual quotes per week going down, and the support tickets per integration staying flat — if the integration generates tickets, the contract was too vague.

And what I would refuse, politely, with an alternative: real-time write access, unbounded historical queries, or a per-client custom endpoint that only one carrier uses. Those three are where integration projects go to die.

Say it like this: “Read out, never write in, with a documented contract, quotas and per-client credentials — the win is fewer manual quotes, and the guardrail is that an external system never drives our pricing.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Contract-first OpenAPI, OAuth client credentials scoped per carrier, rate limiting and a documented error model — versioned so a partner's integration does not break when we ship.”
If he pushes: What SLA would you commit? · How do you handle an outage on their side? · Would you charge for the integration?
Q17 JD: multi-layered .NET, SoC, DI, ORM Rules and disputes

“Multi-layered .NET, separation of concerns, dependency injection, ORM.” Two carriers calculate the same charge differently — how do you keep that maintainable?

Question: “Two carriers insist on different calculations for what is nominally the same charge. Both are right for their business. How do you build that without the code becoming unmaintainable?”
What he is really testing: whether layering means something to you operationally — specifically that a calculation rule can be isolated, tested and traced to the shipment it priced.
Show answer · rule as a policy, per-carrier configuration, audit, what to avoid

“The calculation is the product, so it should live in one place, be named after the rule, and be replaceable per carrier without touching the rest of the system.”

  • Layer it. Rates arrive through an API layer, the calculation sits in a domain layer that knows nothing about HTTP or the database, and persistence sits behind an interface. That separation is what lets me test a rule without a server or a database.
  • Make the rule a policy, not an if. One implementation per carrier-specific rule, selected by configuration. Adding a carrier becomes a new class plus its test cases, not an edit to a method that nine carriers already depend on.
  • Keep the evidence. Every priced shipment records which rule version produced the number and the inputs used. When an invoice is disputed six weeks later, the answer is a lookup, not an investigation — and that is a client-facing benefit, not a technical nicety.
  • Avoid the two failure modes. A giant conditional inside a shared method is unmaintainable after the third carrier; a fork of the product per client is unmaintainable after the second. Configuration with tests is the narrow path between them.

The business outcome is the one to say out loud: dispute resolution goes from days to minutes, onboarding a new carrier becomes configuration instead of a project, and a rule change can be released for one client without a regression risk for the others.

Say it like this: “The calculation is the product: one rule per carrier as a policy, selected by configuration, with the rule version recorded on every priced shipment — so a dispute is a lookup, not an investigation.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Domain layer with no framework dependency, rule implementations behind an interface and registered in DI, EF Core or Dapper at the edge, and golden cases per carrier in the test suite.”
If he pushes: What if the client's rule changes mid-contract? · How do you test rules nobody documented? · Where do you draw the line on configurability?
Q18 JD: authentication and authorization Audits

“Develop secure authentication and authorization.” A carrier's auditor asks who changed a rate, and when. Can the system answer?

Question: “Security in the posting. In client terms: an auditor arrives and asks who changed a rate last quarter, from which office, and under what approval. What does the client need from us?”
What he is really testing: whether security means “login works” to you or “the evidence exists”. For carriers, auditability of commercial decisions is a real cost line.
Show answer · identity, least privilege, audit trail, the audit-week saving

“Two different things bundled together: who is allowed in, and what we can prove afterwards.”

  • Identity through their own provider. Single sign-on over OIDC where the carrier already has an identity provider means joiners and leavers are handled by their HR process, not by us emailing passwords. Fewer shared accounts is the cheapest security win and it removes a recurring audit finding.
  • Least privilege that matches the organisation. Roles by function and by office or region, so a pricing analyst in one market cannot edit another's rates. Access reviews then take an hour instead of a week.
  • An audit trail of commercial changes. Every rate, allocation or allotment change records who, when, from where, the previous value, the new value and the reason. That is one immutable log, written in the same transaction as the change — a log written afterwards is a log that will be wrong.
  • A report the auditor can read. If answering the question requires an engineer to write a query, the client pays for it every audit cycle.

The business case is not abstract: audit preparation drops from days of emails to a filtered report, disputes about a rate change get answered from evidence, and access reviews stop being a project. I would put it to a client exactly that way, and I would volunteer that the log itself can never be edited — otherwise the evidence is worth nothing.

Say it like this: “Two things: who gets in, and what we can prove afterwards. SSO with their provider, least privilege by office, and an immutable log of every commercial change written with the change itself.”
One line of proof, if asked: “OIDC with the carrier's identity provider, policy-based authorization per function and region, and the audit row committed in the same transaction as the update.”
If he pushes: What if the client has no identity provider? · How long do you keep the log? · Where does personal data come into this?
Q19 JD: databases, performance, reliability Operational cost

“Optimize databases, performance and reliability.” The monthly close gets slower every year. What do you do about it?

Question: “A client says the month-end process took six hours last year and nine this year, and it will keep growing. They want it back under four. How do you approach that?”
What he is really testing: whether you treat performance as an operational cost with a business deadline, and whether you measure before proposing anything.
Show answer · stage timings, data access, archiving, a performance budget

“A monthly process that degrades predictably is a data-growth problem, not a mystery. I would profile the stages before proposing anything, because the fix is different if the time is in extraction, calculation or reporting.”

  • Stage timings first. Extract, transform, calculate, publish — with row counts. Most of these runs lose their time in data movement and per-record logic, not in the calculation itself.
  • Fix the data access. Set-based statements instead of row-by-row loops, correct indexes for the filters actually used, and no query that loads a whole table to use twenty rows.
  • Reduce the work. Recalculate only what changed, aggregate incrementally instead of from scratch, and archive closed periods out of the hot path. The cheapest month-end is the one that does less.
  • Protect the gain. A performance budget for the process, tested against a realistic data volume, and an alert when a stage exceeds it — otherwise the next three features quietly give the time back.

The business framing matters: this is finance overtime, decisions made on yesterday's numbers, and a client who starts asking whether the platform can cope. Getting the close to four hours is worth more in confidence than any new screen — and it is usually achievable without touching the calculation logic at all.

Say it like this: “Nine hours growing every year is a data-growth problem: profile the stages, fix the data access, do less work by recalculating only what changed, and set a budget so the gain is not lost.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Set-based SQL over per-row updates, keyset pagination instead of large offsets, partition or archive closed periods, and a performance test in the pipeline at production-like volume.”
If he pushes: What if the bottleneck is a third-party system? · Would you ever move this to a different database? · How do you prove the numbers still reconcile after optimising?
Q20 JD: AI tools, reviews, deployments, incidents Speed versus risk

“AI-assisted development, peer reviews, production deployments and incident resolution.” How do you go faster without becoming riskier?

Question: “The posting asks for AI-assisted development, code reviews, deployments and incident support. Put together: how do you increase delivery speed in a small team without increasing the number of incidents our clients see?”
What he is really testing: whether you understand that speed and safety are produced by the same mechanisms — small changes, automated checks and the ability to reverse — rather than by working harder.
Show answer · small batches, automated gates, AI with review, reversibility

“Speed comes from small changes and reversibility; safety comes from the same two things. AI helps with the volume of work around the change, not with the judgement about releasing it.”

  • Small batches. A change a colleague can review in twenty minutes ships the same day. A change that takes a week to review is where risk and delay both accumulate.
  • Gates that cannot be skipped. Build, unit and integration tests, static analysis and a security scan, enforced by branch policy rather than by good intentions. If a check can be bypassed under pressure, it will be — on the worst possible day.
  • AI where it is draft work. Test cases, documentation, log summaries, a first version of a query or a refactor. Reviewed line by line, with a test that fails if the fix is removed. It buys hours per week; it does not decide whether a pricing rule is correct.
  • Reversibility by design. Feature flags, expand-then-contract database changes, and a rollback that one person can perform at night without a meeting. Most incidents become small when going back is easy.
  • Incidents as input. Every incident ends with one missing check and one runbook line, which is how the same failure stops recurring without blaming anyone.

I would report three numbers to a VP rather than a story about velocity: time from request to production, change failure rate, and time to restore. If the first falls and the second does not rise, the team is genuinely faster. That combination is what a client feels as reliability.

Say it like this: “Small batches, gates nobody can skip, and every change reversible — with AI drafting the work around the change. I would report lead time, change failure rate and time to restore, not a velocity story.”
One line of proof, if asked: “Trunk-based work with short branches, a pipeline that blocks the merge on red, feature flags per capability, and a rollback step written in the runbook before the release.”
If he pushes: What would you automate first? · How do you convince a team that has been burned by releases? · Where do you still allow manual approval?
