Airline Revenue Management, explained from first principles

Revenue management is the science and strategy behind how an airline decides ticket prices, allocates seats and extracts the most value from a flight that will depart whether or not anyone buys the last seat. This note builds the idea step by step: why the economics make it necessary, what the metrics mean, how demand is forecast, how prices and inventory are set, why overbooking exists, what changes in a network and what changes again in cargo. Every formula is derived and every chart uses one consistent example.

1 · What revenue management isThe four “rights” and the three decisions behind them

“Sell the right seat to the right customer at the right price at the right time.” It is a good definition because it names four variables, and each one is a real decision that has to be made before departure: which unit of capacity, which customer segment, which price, which moment.

The discipline emerged in the 1980s, when deregulation let airlines set their own fares and forced them to compete on price instead of on a regulated schedule. Two things then became visible at once: a seat that departs empty has no residual value, and different customers are willing to pay very different amounts for the same seat. Managing that gap is the whole business.

Underneath the four rights sit three decisions that the rest of this note develops:

ForecastHow much demand will appear, at each price, and when.
PriceWhat to charge, and how to fence the segments apart.
AllocationHow much capacity to protect for demand that has not arrived yet.

A fourth decision — overbooking — belongs to the same family: how many bookings to accept when some of them will not turn up.

2 · Why it exists at allFour economic conditions that make revenue management necessary

1
Capacity is fixed and expensive to change. An aircraft has a known number of seats (or tonnes) months in advance; adding capacity means a different aircraft or a different schedule, not a production line.
2
The unit is perishable. At departure the seat expires. Inventory does not carry over, so the cost of an empty seat is the revenue it could have earned — not its physical cost.
3
Marginal cost is close to zero. Carrying one more passenger costs a little fuel, a meal and a bag; the flight itself is already paid for. So almost any fare above marginal cost contributes to covering fixed cost.
4
Demand is segmented and arrives over time. Leisure travellers book early and are price sensitive; business travellers book late and pay for flexibility. They compete for the same seat, and they arrive in a predictable order.

The consequence: if you sell the first seats to whoever asks, the cheap early demand consumes the capacity that the expensive late demand would have paid for. Revenue management exists to hold capacity back — deliberately, with a number attached to the decision.

3 · Metrics and identitiesYield, load factor and RASK are one equation, not three ideas

Three quantities appear in every revenue discussion, and they are related by an identity that is worth memorising because it explains most arguments about “we need higher load factors”.

  • RPK — revenue passenger kilometres: passengers × distance. What was actually sold.
  • ASK — available seat kilometres: seats × distance. What was offered.
  • Revenue — the money collected.
Definitions
Load factor = RPKASK    Yield = RevenueRPK    RASK = RevenueASK

Now substitute: revenue = yield × RPK, and RPK = load factor × ASK. Divide both sides by ASK and the identity appears.

The identity, derived
RASK = RevenueASK = Yield × RPKASK = Yield × RPKASK = Yield × Load factor

That single line settles a recurring argument. A flight can fly fuller and earn less, because the extra passengers were cheap. Two airlines with the same aircraft can have the same revenue per available seat kilometre while one of them carries far more people — or the same load factor with very different revenue.

For cargo the same logic runs on different units: revenue per kilogram or per tonne-kilometre, and a “load factor” that has to be measured in both weight and volume, because a full hold by volume can still be underweight.

4 · Forecasting and the booking curveDemand is not what you sold — and the difference is the whole discipline

Bookings accumulate over months, not at a single moment. Plot cumulative bookings against days before departure and you get the booking curve: slow at first, steep in the last weeks, flattening at the end. Each point on that curve is a forecast problem — how much more will arrive from here?

The censoring problem

Here is the subtlety that separates a real forecast from a spreadsheet: what you sold is not what was demanded. Bookings are capped by the availability you published. If the cheap bucket closed, the missing demand is invisible — and if you forecast from bookings alone, the model learns your own restrictions and quietly ratifies them.

Observation model
Bobserved = min(D, A)   where D is true demand and A is the availability offered
so when Bobserved = A, the observation is censored and D ≥ A is all we know

Unconstraining means estimating the hidden part — with refused/regret data where it exists, with statistical corrections where it does not. The practical test of any method: it must reproduce the demand of departures that were not constrained, and it must raise its estimate when availability was tight.

Projecting the rest of the curve

Once each class on each departure has a history of booking curves, the simplest usable model is exponential smoothing on the pick-up increments:

Exponential smoothing
Ft+1 = α·At + (1 − α)·Ft,   0 < α ≤ 1
α is the weight given to the newest observation. α near 1 tracks shocks and noise; α near 0 is stable and slow. The right value is chosen by backtesting, not by taste.

Then the remaining demand is the sum of expected increments between now and departure, and forecast quality is judged on two numbers that answer different questions:

Forecast quality
MAPE = 1n Σ |Fi − Ai|Ai     Bias = 1n Σ Fi − AiAi
MAPE measures the size of the miss; bias measures its direction. A forecast that is consistently 4% low has an acceptable MAPE and still leaks revenue on every departure.

What to remember: a forecast is not a number, it is a distribution. Every decision that follows — protection levels, bid prices, overbooking — needs the spread as much as the mean, which is why the normal approximation with a mean and a standard deviation appears in the next three sections.

5 · Pricing and elasticityWhy the revenue-maximising price is not always the right price

The second decision is the price itself. Demand responds to price, and the response is summarised by elasticity:

Price elasticity of demand
ε = ΔQ / QΔP / P
|ε| > 1: demand is elastic, a 1% price rise loses more than 1% of volume, so revenue falls. |ε| < 1: inelastic, a price rise gains revenue. Revenue is maximised where |ε| = 1.

Take a simple linear demand curve, Q = 300 − 3P. Revenue is R = P·Q = 300P − 3P², a parabola whose maximum sits where the derivative is zero:

Revenue-maximising price
dRdP = 300 − 6P = 0  ⇒  P* = 50,   Q* = 150,   R* = 7,500

Now add the constraint that makes this an airline problem rather than a pricing exercise: the aircraft has 120 seats. Selling 150 at 50 is not available. The best price that still fills exactly the capacity available is higher, and the revenue is lower — the constraint costs money, and revenue management is the discipline of recovering as much of it as possible by selling to different customers at different prices instead of one price to everybody.

The recovery is segmentation: fences — advance purchase, Saturday stay, change restrictions, refundability — separate customers by willingness to pay, so the same seat is sold at several prices without one customer simply buying the cheaper one. The transcript's example is this mechanism in miniature: hold a few cheap seats early, then raise the price as the flight fills.

6 · Inventory control: Littlewood and EMSRHow many seats to protect for demand that has not arrived

Pricing decides the menu; allocation decides how much of the aircraft is available at each price. The classic setting is two fare classes on one leg: a cheap fare fL that is available now, and a high fare fH whose demand arrives later and is uncertain.

Protect one more seat for the high fare and you gain fH if that demand materialises, or nothing if it does not:

Marginal value of protecting a seat
E[marginal revenue] = fH · P(DH > y)
y is the protection level: seats held back from the cheap fare. As y grows, the probability that high-fare demand exceeds it falls, so the marginal value falls.

Sell the seat cheaply instead and you receive fL with certainty. Accept the cheap booking only while the certain money beats the expected money:

Littlewood's rule (1972)
accept the low fare if   fL ≥ fH · P(DH > y)
the optimal protection level y* satisfies   P(DH > y*) = fLfH
With normal demand DH ~ N(μ, σ²): y* = μ + σ·Φ⁻¹(1 − fL/fH).

Numerically: 180 seats, fH = 300, fL = 120, and high-fare demand N(40, 12²). The critical ratio is 120/300 = 0.40, so Φ⁻¹(0.60) = 0.253 and y* = 40 + 0.253 × 12 ≈ 43 seats protected, leaving 137 sellable at the low fare.

From two classes to five: EMSR-b

Real cabins have several fares. EMSR-b applies Littlewood repeatedly, but treats all the classes above a given class as one aggregate competitor with a revenue-weighted average fare:

EMSR-b protection level for the class below j
Sj = Σi<j fi·μi    μj = Σi<j μi    σj = √(Σi<j σi²)    f̄j = Sjμj
yj = μj + σj·Φ⁻¹(1 − fj+1 / f̄j)
Booking limit for the lower class = capacity − protection level above it. Limits nest: a booking at the cheap fare consumes capacity available to every higher fare too.

Second example, a 180-seat cabin with four fares (Y 400, M 250, K 150, L 90) and demand means 18, 25, 35 and open-ended respectively, with standard deviations 6, 8 and 10:

StepAggregate aboveμσf̄fbelow/f̄Protection yBooking limit
Protect against LY + M + K7814.1239.70.3768397
Protect against KY + M4310.0312.80.48044136
Protect against MY186.0400.00.62516164

Read the table as a policy: sell L while total bookings are below 97; between 97 and 136 only K and above are open; between 136 and 164 only M and above; above 164 only Y. The whole inventory policy of a departure is three numbers.

What breaks it: EMSR-b assumes independent demand between classes and a single leg. Both assumptions are wrong in a network — the same seat serves several O&D markets — which is exactly why network optimisation exists.

7 · OverbookingTrading a certain small gain against an uncertain large cost

Not everyone who books turns up. Some cancel, some simply do not appear. If the airline sells exactly the number of seats, it expects to depart with empty seats — so it sells more. The decision is not “should we overbook” but “how far”, and that question has a clean answer from inventory theory.

Let capacity be C, bookings B, and each booking's show probability p, independent. Then the number of passengers who appear is binomial:

Show-ups
XB ~ Binomial(B, p)    E[XB] = B·p    Var[XB] = B·p·(1 − p)
For B above about 30 the normal approximation with μ = Bp and σ = √(Bp(1−p)) is accurate enough for the decision.

Two costs pull in opposite directions:

  • Spoilage cost Cs: the value lost when a seat departs empty because we refused a booking.
  • Denial cost Cd: compensation, rebooking, hotel and goodwill when a passenger is denied boarding.
Expected cost of a booking level B
EC(B) = Cd·E[(XB − C)+] + Cs·E[(C − XB)+]
The first term is the cost of having too many show up; the second is the cost of having too few.

This is the newsvendor problem with the signs reversed, so the optimality condition is the critical fractile:

Optimal overbooking level
P(XB* ≤ C) = CdCd + Cs   ⇔   P(XB* > C) = CsCd + Cs
Denial expensive ⇒ the ratio approaches 1 ⇒ sell little beyond capacity. Spoilage expensive ⇒ the ratio approaches 0 ⇒ sell well beyond it.

Worked numerically: C = 150, p = 0.92, Cs = 200, Cd = 900. The critical fractile is 900/1,100 = 0.818. Searching B: at 159 bookings μ = 146.3, σ = 3.42, so P(X ≤ 150) ≈ Φ(1.08) = 0.86; at 160, P ≈ Φ(0.82) = 0.79. The optimum sits at about 160 bookings — roughly ten seats of deliberate overbooking.

In cargo the same logic is weaker and stranger. A shipment that does not appear leaves space that may still be filled by another shipment, and “no-show” cargo often simply rolls to the next flight. Overbooking exists, but it is negotiated through allotments more than calculated through a fractile.

8 · Network revenue managementWhen the seat is shared by several markets, the leg is the wrong unit

On a connecting network, one seat on leg A–H is also part of the product A–B. Selling it to a local A–H passenger may block a much more valuable A–B passenger. Leg-based controls cannot see that; network controls price the leg by what it enables.

The linear program and its duals

Network allocation is a packing problem: choose how much of each market to accept so that no leg is oversold.

Network allocation LP
max Σk fk·xk    subject to   Σk aik·xk ≤ Ci for every leg i,   0 ≤ xk ≤ Dk
fk is the fare of market k, xk the quantity accepted, aik = 1 if market k uses leg i, Ci the leg capacity, Dk the demand.

Every capacity constraint has a dual price πi ≥ 0: the marginal value of one more seat on that leg. Those duals are the bid prices, and they turn the optimisation into a rule a reservation system can apply in milliseconds:

Acceptance rule
accept request k if   fk ≥ Σi∈k πi
A request that uses two legs must beat the sum of both shadow prices. This is why the same seat has a different minimum price depending on the itinerary.

Worked example

MarketFareLegs usedDemandLeg-based testNetwork test
A–H local180A–H200180 ≥ 180 → accept180 ≥ 180 → accept
H–B local150H–B200150 ≥ 150 → accept150 ≥ 150 → accept
A–B through320A–H + H–B100320 ≥ 150 → accept320 < 330 → reject

The comparison is instructive because both answers look defensible until you do the arithmetic:

  • Locals only: 120 × 180 + 100 × 150 = 21,600 + 15,000 = 36,600.
  • Through first: 100 × 320 = 32,000, plus the 20 seats left on A–H at 180 = 3,600 → 35,600.

A leg-based rule accepts the 320 through fare because it beats the 150 local fare on the second leg; it loses 1,000. The network rule rejects it, because the through passenger's true opportunity cost is 330. Raise the through fare to 360 and the decision flips: 100 × 360 + 20 × 180 = 39,600, so accept.

The insight to carry into a conversation: a bid price is not a price list and not a cost. It is the marginal value of capacity, and it changes as the booking horizon advances — which is why the same fare can be accepted in the morning and rejected at night.

9 · Cargo: when the unit is a kilogramTwo constraints, chargeable weight, and commitments that outrank the optimiser

Cargo revenue management keeps the same loop — forecast, price, allocate, measure — but changes the unit of inventory. Capacity is consumed in kilograms and cubic metres at the same time, and often in container positions on the deck. Whichever runs out first is the real constraint.

Chargeable weight
Wc = max(Wactual, V6,000)    with V in cm³ and W in kg
The divisor is the industry volumetric factor: 6,000 cm³ per kilogram, i.e. one cubic metre ≈ 166.7 kg. Light, bulky cargo pays for the space it occupies rather than the weight it registers.
Two-dimensional capacity constraint
Σk wk·xk ≤ Wmax    and    Σk vk·xk ≤ Vmax
Both constraints must hold for every leg. A shipment that is fine on weight can still be refused because the hold is full of light cargo.

Two commercial structures then sit on top of the optimisation:

  • Allotments and block-space agreements. Capacity contractually reserved for a customer, used or not. Free sale is what remains. Any availability logic must respect the hierarchy — committed space first, then contracts with priority, then free sale — because selling into committed space is a contract breach, not an optimisation.
  • Rating. The price is built from a tariff with minimum charges, weight breaks and pivot weights. It must be deterministic and reproducible: six weeks later, a disputed invoice has to recompute to the same number from the same inputs.
Rating with a pivot weight
charge = max(Wc·rnormal, P·rpivot, minimum charge)
Below the pivot weight the higher rate applies and the charge scales with the pivot; above it the normal rate takes over. This is why a heavier shipment can cost less than a lighter one.

10 · From theory to productionWhy the model is the easy part

In a live system the optimisation is a small step inside a daily cycle, and most failures happen around it rather than inside it.

What tends to break, in order of frequency:

  • Data arriving late or short. A schedule file missing a hundred flights must fail the run, not become a plan with invisible gaps.
  • Unconstrained demand computed from constrained observations. The feedback loop described in section 4, invisible in a demo and expensive over a year.
  • Optimiser output nobody can explain. A planner who cannot see why a bid price moved will switch the control off. Explainability is a production requirement, not a nice-to-have.
  • Latency at the availability layer. Controls are useless if the query path cannot return them in milliseconds; caching and pre-computation belong in the design from the start.

The protections come from the EMSR-b formula in section 6: against L, protect 111 (limit 39); against K, protect 61 (limit 89); against M, protect 22 (limit 128). If every class sells to its limit, the flight earns 29,560 — an average of 197 per seat.

The value of the policy, in one comparison
selling all 150 seats at the cheapest fare: 150 × 90 = 13,500
selling under the protection levels: 29,560   (2.2× more, same aircraft, same seats)
That gap is the entire business case for revenue management, and it is also its risk: the policy earns more only if the high-fare demand actually appears. If it does not, the protected seats depart empty.

Add the overbooking decision from section 7: with a 92% show rate and costs of 200 for spoilage and 900 for denial, the optimal booking level is about 160 — ten seats above capacity, accepting a small probability of denying boarding to avoid a much larger probability of flying with empty seats.

13 · Sources and caveatsWhat this note is, and what it is not

Primary source. This note expands the transcript “What Is Airline Revenue Management?” (Global Insights 24), which introduces the definition, the 1980s deregulation context, forecasting, pricing, inventory control, segmentation and overbooking. The transcript is an overview; the mathematics, examples and charts here are built around it to make the concepts teachable.

Standard results used. Littlewood's rule (1972) for two-class protection; EMSR and EMSR-b (Belobaba, 1989) for multi-class protection; the newsvendor critical fractile for overbooking; the network allocation LP and its dual prices for bid-price control. These are textbook results, presented here with their derivations rather than as citations of authority.

Caveats. All numbers are illustrative and internally consistent but not taken from an airline. Real implementations add constraints this note simplifies: time-dependent demand, competition, fare rules, connecting itineraries, cargo allotments, and forecasting error that is correlated across departures. Treat the formulas as the shape of the reasoning, not as an implementation specification.
